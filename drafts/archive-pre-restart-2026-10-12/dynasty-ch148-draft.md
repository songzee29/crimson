# Chapter 148 — "Kaldrek Vane"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

Kaldrek Vane did not wait to be found.

That was the difference between him and the others, and it almost — *almost* — saved him. The morning after Vaeren Thoss died on Royal Street, Vane emptied his accounts, burned three safe houses, and fled New Orleans the way a rich man flees anything: first class, window seat, a name that wasn't his on a ticket bought with money that was.

Zee let him run for nine days.

He let him run because the list had said Vane was the money — the one who'd paid the eight at the harbor, who'd laundered Jorren Vale's gold through four shell chapels and a funeral home — and Zee wanted him to feel, for a little while, the particular hope of a man who thinks distance is a kind of armor. He wanted Vane to land somewhere far away and exhale.

He wanted the exhale.

On the tenth day, Zee found him in a hotel room in a city whose name doesn't matter, because Kaldrek Vane's money had bought him a suite on the forty-first floor and Zee walked in through the wall.

Not through the door. Through the *wall* — the vampire in him moving faster than the eye's contract with the world allows, the witch in him unmaking the plaster and the steel and the silence in one small, surgical thought, the wolf in him already reading the room: two bodyguards, one accountant with a gun he'd never fired, Vane himself in a silk robe with a glass of something amber, standing at the window looking at a skyline he'd paid to see.

The wall resealed behind Zee like water closing over a stone.

"Evening," Zee said.

The bodyguards moved. They were good — ex-military, ex-something, the kind of men whose hands don't shake.

It didn't matter.

Zee was across the room before the first one cleared leather, and his claws went in under the man's jaw and *up*, through the soft floor of the mouth, through the tongue, the points punching up into the brainpan — and the bodyguard's whole body went rigid and then slack, blood pouring out of his mouth in a red sheet down his shirt, and Zee let him fall.

The second one got a shot off. It went wide, wild, through the window — glass starred, night air screaming in — and then Zee had him by the front of his jacket and *lifted* him, and the other hand went through his ribs from the front, claws spreading them like a butcher spreading a carcass, and Zee closed his fist around the man's heart and tore it *out*. It beat twice in his hand, shocked and obscene, spraying arterial red across the marble, across Zee's coat, across the skyline in the broken window — and then it stopped, and Zee dropped it, and it hit the floor with a wet sound like a dropped fruit.

He let the bodyguard's body follow it.

The suite stank of blood and gunpowder and piss. The accountant had dropped his gun and put his hands on his head and started praying to a god Zee had never met, urine spreading dark across the front of his trousers.

"Don't," Zee told him, not unkindly. "He's busy."

Vane hadn't moved from the window. Blood flecked the silk of his robe. That was interesting. Zee filed it away.

"You let me run," Vane said. He didn't turn around. "Nine days. I counted."

"I know."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted you to choose the room." Zee gestured with a bloodied hand at the suite — the marble, the broken window, the amber glass still somehow upright on the sideboard. "Men die better in rooms they chose. It's one of my few superstitions."

Vane finally turned. He was a soft-looking man, mid-fifties, the softness of a life spent converting other men's violence into numbers. But his eyes were clear. Clear eyes in a soft face — that was the money man's whole trick, and Zee had seen the trick before, in better men than this one.

"I have information," Vane said.

"I know."

"It's worth more than my life."

"Nothing is worth more than your life," Zee said. "That's the first thing the dying teach you. Say it anyway."

Vane set down his glass. His hand was steady. Zee gave him credit for the hand.

"Jorren Vale didn't hire the eight to kill you," Vane said. "That was the contract on paper. The real contract was different. Vale was *bidding* — he was selling the highest bidder a demonstration. Eight men, one harbor, one unkillable king. And the bidder was your father."

The accountant made a small sound, wet and broken.

"Victor Darkheart," Vane said, "paid for a *test*. Not a kill. The order was explicit — 'testing, not killing.' He wanted to see what you'd do when you were jumped. How fast the natures answer. Whether the wolf hunts or the witch writes or the vampire—" He stopped. Swallowed. His eyes flicked to the blood on Zee's mouth, the heart on the marble. "Whether the vampire *eats*."

Zee stood very still. The crimson-black horns were out. The scales had come up the side of his throat like a tide of dark glass, and there was *heat* under them now — the dragon blood answering, interested, awake in a way it hadn't been on Royal Street.

"And the Zerlin approach," Zee said.

Vane's clear eyes flickered. "You know the name."

"I know the shape of it." Zee took a step closer. The air in the room bent faintly around him, the way air bends around a fire. "Hunger. Control. The dragon blood my father is so frightened of. Tell me the rest."

"There's a window," Vane said quickly. "Victor's people found it — a *window* in you, a stretch of days where the vampire hunger will peak and the wolf will fight it and the dragon—" He shook his head. "They don't understand the dragon part. Nobody does. But they know the hunger is coming, and Victor wants to be watching when it arrives. He wants to see if the king can be *starved on command*. If you can be made to—" He gestured helplessly at the blood on the floor, the heart, Zee's mouth. "To choose. Hunger or family. That's the test. That's the Zerlin approach."

Silence. The skyline glittered through the broken window. The accountant had stopped praying and started crying, quietly, into his own hands.

Zee considered the man in the blood-flecked silk robe. Considered the information — which matched the list, which matched the shape Zee had already felt forming under his own skin, the low cello note of the hunger tuning itself in the next room, louder since Royal Street, louder since Thoss's blood. Considered the nine days of running, the window seat, the exhale.

"You're wondering," Vane said, "whether the information buys me anything."

"I'm wondering," Zee said, "whether you believed it would."

Vane smiled then — a real smile, sad and almost admiring. "I believed it was the only card I had. A money man always plays the hand he's dealt."

"Then you should know," Zee said, stepping in close, close enough to see the individual gray hairs in the man's eyebrows, close enough that Vane could smell the blood on him, "that the hand was marked. Victor used you the way he used the eight — as instruments. He'll use the Zerlin window the same way. And when the window opens, and the hunger comes, and he stands there watching to see what I choose—"

The crimson light came up in Zee's eyes like a dawn that had decided to be a wildfire.

"—he's going to learn what everyone learns."

Zee put his hand on Kaldrek Vane's chest.

"That I was never the thing being tested."

The claws went in through the silk and the skin and the meat, ribs cracking outward like a ribcage unhinging, and Zee bent his head to the wound and *fed* — mouth to the torn chest, drinking straight from the heart's own door while it still beat against his tongue.

Vane made a sound — not a scream, something lower, something animal — and his clear eyes went wide and then *unfocused*, the cleverness draining out of them like water out of a cracked glass. His hands came up and clutched at Zee's shoulders, weakly, the strength going out of them by the second.

And the hunger *roared*.

It came up through Zee like a tide coming in, vast and exultant, and this time he didn't leash it fast — this time he let it run three heartbeats longer than he had on Royal Street, let it *have* the kill the way it wanted. The dragon blood answered with a surge of heat that rolled off his scales in visible waves, the air shimmering, the broken window's edges sweating — and then the deeper fire answered *with* it, the black-crimson blaze from Royal Street, rising through the dragon-heat like a second flame inside the first. It spread across Zee's shoulders in great wings of shadow and ember, and Vane's unfocusing eyes found the burning dark above him — and whatever he saw in it took the last of the cleverness out of his face. Not fear. Not anymore. *Awe.*

The suite's sprinklers burst, all of them at once, as if the room itself were trying to put him out — rain hammering down, bursting to steam where it struck the burning wings, the air filling with the smell of scorch and black sugar, feather-shapes of vapor curling up and dying around them both.

Vane's heart beat twice more against Zee's mouth, uneven, and stopped.

Zee straightened slowly, water streaming off his horns, blood diluted pink running down his chin and throat. Vane's body hung in his grip a moment longer, emptied, the silk robe soaked red-black — and then Zee laid him down on the marble beside his heart, almost gently, and closed his eyes.

The hunger coiled under his ribs, sated and *singing* — and beneath the singing, the black fire banked itself down to embers, patient, waiting to be wanted again. That was the terrible part: that it was worse sated than starving, that every feeding taught it the shape of being fed, that Victor's window wasn't coming someday.

It was opening *now*, from the inside, and Zee could feel the edges of it — and at the edges, embers.

He turned to the accountant, who had gone rigid with terror, soaked and shaking under the sprinklers.

"What's your name?" Zee asked. Water ran off his horns. Blood ran pink off his hands.

"—P-Pell. Neris Pell."

Zee went very still.

Of course. Of *course* the money had an accountant, and of course the accountant had a name from the list. Neris Pell — fifth on the remaining order, after Sera Quill and Jorren Vale. Sitting here on a hotel carpet with his hands on his head, soaked, weeping into his own palms, piss-dark trousers, holding the ledgers of the eight.

Zee looked at him for a long moment. The wolf in his blood stirred, interested — the small ones were easy, the small ones barely counted as hunting. The vampire tasted the fear and found it thin and sour — the fear of a clerk, not a blade, not a threat, just a man who'd done arithmetic for murderers.

Zee's law was simple: *do not hurt my own people. Threaten them — you die. No investigation first.* But Pell hadn't threatened anyone. Pell had *added*. And there was a difference, and Zee's code — older and meaner than any man's, but a code — knew the difference.

"Listen to me, Neris Pell," Zee said, and the accountant looked up with drowned eyes. "You are going to live. Do you understand why?"

Pell shook his head, mute.

"Because you never held a knife. You held a pen." Zee crouched, bringing himself to the man's level, water dripping off his chin onto the marble between them. "But the pen has a price too. Here's your price: you're going to take every ledger you have — every account, every shell chapel, every funeral home, every dollar Jorren Vale ever moved through you — and you're going to deliver it to House Vesper. To Lucien Vesper himself. You'll tell him Zee Darkheart sent you, and that the price of your life is *everything*."

"I— I don't know where—"

"You'll find it." Zee stood. The sprinklers were slowing, dying. Steam rose off his shoulders. "The Vespers have an oath, Pell. They protect my line. Right now, that means they protect the paper trail that leads to my father's door. If you run, the wolf finds you. If you lie, the witch finds it. If you deliver — you live, and you never add another column for another killer again."

Pell nodded, frantic, weeping, alive.

Zee walked to the broken window and looked out at the skyline Vane had paid to see, and felt the hunger move under his ribs like something turning over in its sleep — sated, patient, *waiting* — and thought about Victor, thirty-four years of watching, of studying, of waiting for a window, and thought about Ner at home with his gold mana turned down low, and his sons asleep in the soft dark, and Rez keeping the far watch the way Rez always had, the way a best friend does when the king goes out counting.

*Three,* he thought. *Selk Varren. Brannic Dor. Vaeren Thoss. Kaldrek Vane.*

*Four.*

He left through the wall the way he'd come, and the wall closed behind him like water, and the room kept its dead and its living, and the rain — hundreds of miles south, over Royal Street — kept falling on the wards carved into the streets of New Orleans, humming faint, blue, patient, waiting for the king to come home.
