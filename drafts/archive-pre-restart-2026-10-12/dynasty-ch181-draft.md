# Chapter 181 — "The Rota's First Noble"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The petition arrived at the ninth bell, sealed with a house wax, delivered by a man old enough to have carried the seal when the house was worth more than the seal.

Tessa read it at the war table with Zee's warrant book open beside her, the weekly lines waiting for his hand, and the color left her face the way it always did when paper outran her.

"It's not a claimant," she said. "It's not a creditor. Zee — it's a *petition*."

The marshals had seen it first. The war-council had already debated it. The registry's clerks had made a copy for the consolidated docket before the original ever reached the house, because that was the shape of things now: the King's appetite was a docketed thing, and petitions about it filed themselves.

The wax was a Crown house's. House Verrain — old coin, old stone, a name that sat in the Crowns' seating the way a knife sits in a drawer.

The petitioner wrote in his own hand, in the grammar of a man who had practiced the sentence until the practice wore off it:

*I am Rolen Verrain, second son of House Verrain. I ask to die by the King's hand, on the King's rota, witnessed, as an honor. Voluntary. Penitent. In my own name.*

Rez read it over Tessa's shoulder. His blue-white calm did its work on the room, lowering the temperature by being in it. "Second son," he said. "He wrote it himself. Not the house's clerk. His own hand."

"Why?" Zee asked. He was standing at the window with his back to the table, and the question came out the way questions came out of him now — flat, exact, the tribrid stillness banked underneath it.

"His house brought his father's physician's certificate with the petition," Tessa said. She laid the second page down. "A wasting sickness. A year, perhaps two, and the end of it unkind. He is twenty-six. He watched the rota the way the city watches weather, and he has decided that being taken is better than being unmade." She paused. "He wrote that the marshals file it correctly. *Voluntary, witnessed, penitent.*"

Zee turned from the window. "He wants the rite."

"He wants the honor the condemned got," Rez said. "The court's been calling it that for a season. He read the filing. He believed it."

---

The war-marshal came with the council's reading, which was the reading that mattered first.

"Refuse him," the marshal said, "and the registry files the refusal as policy."

"The registry does that with everything," Zee said.

"Not everything, Majesty." The marshal laid his hand flat on the table, the way men lay hands on tables when the table is the only honest thing in the room. "A man on the rota asks for the rite instead of the rope, and you refuse him — that's a decision about the rite. A condemned man asks, and you refuse him — that's a decision about mercy. But a *titled* man petitions in his own name, of a Crown house, and you refuse *rank* — the registry reads that as policy. A King who will not take noblemen's blood. And then every house in the Crowns' seating has a grievance filed, because a policy of refusal is a policy against them, and policies against houses are things the magistrate can attach to the hearing."

"The hearing is six days out," Tessa said quietly.

"The hearing is six days out," the marshal agreed. "And the advocate will argue, in open session, that the King's Tithe is an unregulated appetite wearing a warrant. A filed refusal of a titled volunteer is the one exhibit the King cannot let him enter — because it proves the appetite is still *his*, still chosen, still a hunger that picks its company by rank." He looked at Zee. "Accept, and the hunger takes a nobleman's blood on schedule, witnessed, filed. Refuse, and the institution becomes a crown that only eats the low."

Zee was very still. "There is no third reading."

"There is," Rez said. The blue-white calm had gone somewhere colder. "Delay. Hold the petition unfiled until after the hearing."

"The house demands a date," the marshal said. "The way houses demand dates for weddings. They've sent a wardrobe note with the petition — the robe, Majesty. The heirloom robe, for the taking." He produced it from his satchel: a folded slip in a steward's hand, itemizing the garment the second son wished to wear. "The waiting list behind him is already forming. Titled names, house seals, each one a claim on the King's appetite with a social calendar attached. Delay one, and the rest file delay as refusal."

Tessa's pen was moving in the warrant book, because the house filed everything, even the conversations that decided the unfileable. The refusal became paper either way. Paper was the only weapon the house had that the Order couldn't take away — and the marshal's reading said the weapon was already pointed at them.

Zee looked at the petition for a long moment — at the practiced hand, the house wax, the physician's certificate, the wardrobe slip.

"Sign it?" Tessa asked. Barely a question.

"Sign it," Zee said.

---

The taking was scheduled for the sixth hour, in the rota chamber, the way the rota took them now: scheduled, witnessed, entered into the court accounts.

They brought him in the heirloom robe. He was thinner than the physician's certificate had suggested, and younger than twenty-six looked on paper, and he walked into the rota chamber the way men walk into chapels — slowly, with the rehearsed calm of a man who has decided the story of his death will be told at his own table.

The gallery filled. That was new. The rota had always been witnessed — marshals, a clerk, the war table's officer — but the witnesses had been functionaries. Now there were house faces in the gallery: Verrain's, and behind Verrain's, others. The waiting list, attending. A titled petitioner was not a procedure. He was a precedent, and the houses had come to watch the precedent be made.

Rolen Verrain knelt when Zee entered. Not because the marshals told him to. Because he had rehearsed it.

"Your Majesty." His voice didn't shake. "I am voluntary. I am penitent. I ask the honor in my own name."

Zee stood over him the way the rota required: close, in the reach of the taking. The clerk stood at his stool with the warrant open — the King's signature already on the weekly line, in the King's own hand, the way the warrants had gone for a season. *Voluntary, witnessed, penitent.* The words were there before the blood was.

The marshals filed it as they always did. The gallery watched as they always hadn't.

"Then it is yours," Zee said.

He took the man's hand first — that was the rite, or the shape of it: the condemned's hand, the King's hand, the way the warrants said it was filed. Rolen's fingers were cold. Not with fear. With the sickness, which had already done half the rota's work and wanted the King's hand to finish the other half and take the credit for the whole.

The hunger came up. It always came up. The vampire in him rose at the nearness of blood the way it had risen since the blackout season — the thing the court had calendarized, the appetite the marshals had learned to schedule and the registry had learned to docket and the war-council had learned to file as logistics. Zee felt it come, and he did not fight it, because fighting it was the blackout and feeding it was the rota, and those were the two truths the Crowns had left him, and he had signed both in his own hand.

He set his mouth to the man's wrist.

The gallery leaned forward. House Verrain watched the King take their son's hand the way the marshals filed it: *voluntary, witnessed, penitent.* The physician's certificate was satisfied. The wardrobe slip was satisfied. The house wax held.

The blood was thin — the sickness had thinned it, the way it thinned everything — but the hunger didn't care about the thinning. The hunger took what was offered, and the offering was honorable, and the honor was the worst of it, because a dying man's dignity had just been fed into the King's appetite and filed as the King's mercy, and every house face in the gallery was doing the arithmetic of what that meant for their own second sons, their own wasting sicknesses, their own heirloom robes.

Rolen's hand went slack in Zee's. The body settled the way bodies settled on the rota — no struggle, by then, because the struggle had been scheduled out of it a season ago.

Zee stood. The clerk wrote the hour. The marshals moved the body the way the rota moved bodies.

In the gallery, the Verrain steward bowed to the King at the exact depth of a man acknowledging a debt paid — the way the preacher had bowed at the recall auction — as if Zee had *bought* the death from them, as if the King's hand had done the house a service and the service would be remembered at the right time, in the right season, with the right reciprocity.

The waiting list filed out behind him. Titled names. House seals. Each one a claim on the King's appetite with a social calendar attached.

---

That night, Tessa closed the warrant book and wrote the weekly line into the consolidated copy, in the registry's hand, the way the rota required: the name, the house, the hour, the filing.

*Voluntary, witnessed, penitent.*

She read it back once, the way the house read everything now — slowly, completely, the way you read a thing that is about to ask something of you.

"The hunger," she said quietly, "is learning to wear a title."

Rez stood by the door — not asked, not thanked, the way he stood by every door now — and said nothing, because there was nothing in the blue-white calm that could file what had happened in the rota chamber.

Zee sat at the war table with the petition, the physician's certificate, the wardrobe slip, and his own signed warrant spread in front of him like a losing hand.

He had signed the rota as penance. A season of weekly signatures, his own hand, so no clerk could ever pretend he didn't know. The institution he had built to end the lies about his hunger.

The hunger had stopped being his secret. It had become a function of state. The monster had gotten a calendar, and everyone had slept better for it — including, worst of all, him.

And now the aristocracy had come with its petitions and its house wax and its heirloom robes, and the institution Zee built as weekly penance had just become the aristocracy's preferred exit.

The calendar didn't have a monster anymore.

The monster had a social season.
