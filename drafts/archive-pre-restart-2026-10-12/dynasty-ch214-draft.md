# Chapter 214 — The Collectible

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

The quarterly tide-tax came due on a still morning, and the treasury did what the file always does when the arithmetic says one thing and the world says another.

It collected anyway.

The collection order went out in the registry's hand, stamped, triplicate, with the auditor's assessment attached — *revenue, in tides* — and the bailiffs came down to the harbor at slack water with rope and seals and a writ, and the writ named the asset the way the law demands assets be named.

Three days of slack water. Standing inventory. Itemized.

They were going to seize the water.

The dredge-master stood on the quay with his crew and watched the bailiffs uncoil their rope, and his wife stood beside him with bread for the men who would not be eating, and nobody on the quay laughed, because the file does not joke and neither does the harbor. The bailiff-master read the order aloud the way the law demands: possession, granted to the Crown, against the tide-tax lien, from the owner.

The owner stood on the quay in a plain coat. Arms folded. The King, attending his own seizure, as the law requires.

Slack water does not seize.

The bailiffs lowered a sealed bucket into the harbor and drew it up, and the water in it was water, and the water was not slack. Slack water is a condition, not a quantity — it is the harbor holding its breath, and a breath does not survive being bucketed. The bailiff-master wrote it down: *attempt one, unsuccessful.* The second bucket: unsuccessful. The third: the water in it was perfectly ordinary harbor water, priced in nothing, owing nothing, and the inventory the file had itemized so carefully — three days, standing, exact — was standing nowhere the bailiffs could put a seal on it.

The file had an assessment. The file had a lien. The file had a collection order. The file had no water.

Then one of the bailiffs — not a treasury man, the King noted, with the part of him that always notes these things; a hire, Order money under the coat — stepped forward with a second writ. Not a seizure writ. A collateral writ.

"By leave of the magistrate," he said, "the Crown attaches the shore-house letter as surety for the collection."

The dredge-master's face changed. The letter — Nhalaris's letter, the prior claim, the shore's first instruction, older than the charter — sat in the rope locker of the lead barge, and this man had just asked the file to take it as payment for a tax the sea owed to a treasury that could not spend what the sea produced.

He reached for the barge.

Zee moved.

It was not fast. That was the worst of it, afterward, in the way the men told it: it was not fast. The King crossed the quay the way water crosses a stone — without hurry and without deviation — and the hire got his hand to the rope locker's latch and no further, because the temperature on the quay changed, and men who have lived near predators know what that means in their marrow.

Zee's rule had never been simpler.

The hire died over the rail, quietly, the way the Order's man had died in Ch 212's dark water — no witnesses but the ones the King always has — and the water took him without a ripple worth the name. The collateral writ fell from his hand and landed on the stones, and Zee looked down at it for one long moment, and then he picked it up, and the bailiffs — the real ones, treasury men, pale now — stood very still.

"The letter," Zee said, to the bailiff-master, "is prior to the charter. You know that. The registry knows that. Your own audit priced the harbor around it." He folded the dead man's writ once, neatly, the way a man folds a thing he is about to file. "The collection proceeds against the inventory named in the order. Not the evidence. Not the prior claim. The inventory."

The bailiff-master swallowed. "The inventory can't be seized."

"I know," Zee said.

And then the King did the thing the file would remember for a hundred years.

He signed the collection as owner.

He took the collection order — the seizure of three days of slack water, standing inventory, from the sea he owned — and he executed it himself. He granted the treasury possession. He delivered the inventory: three days of slack water, in the harbor, unchanged, exactly as itemized. He signed the transfer in triplicate. He signed the receipt. He filed it with the bailiff-master in the registry's hand, on the quay, in front of the crews and the dredge-master's wife and the water.

The collection was complete.

The treasury now held possession of slack water — the whole of it, exactly as assessed — and the file had a precedent it had never had before: the Crown had seized Crown property from its own king, at his signature, in the open, witnessed. The bailiffs carried away their rope and their seals and their filed receipt, and they carried nothing else, because there was nothing else to carry, and the harbor held its breath behind them the way it had always held it.

The dredge-master looked at the King. "What did they take?"

"Possession," Zee said. "They have possession of the inventory. Filed."

"And the water?"

"The water," said the King, "is mine the way the tide is. They have the paper. I have the sea."

He walked up the quay alone, and the clerks recorded the death of the Order's hire as an inventory disturbance, resolved — the machinery filing its own horror and keeping its count — and the receipt for the loss of the King's own sea went into the archive in triplicate, signed, stamped, filed.

The arithmetic of ownership, at the end of it, was exactly what the file had been building toward all year:

The only man who could certify the sea's arrears was the one the Crown was billing for them.

The only man who could grant the Crown possession of the sea's inventory was the one who owned it.

Revenue, in tides. Payable, in signatures.

And the King kept signing, because the shore is the one who signs, and the shore is the one who stays.
