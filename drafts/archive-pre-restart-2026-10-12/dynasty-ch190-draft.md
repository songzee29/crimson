# Chapter 190 — "The Nine-Minute Rule"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The magistrate's ruling on the hearing's procedure came down in chambers, at the morning bell, in the clerk's hand — and the war table read it the way the house read everything now: slowly, completely, the way you read a thing that is about to ask something of you.

Two days to the hearing. The sealed lots sat in the docket, admitted, sealed. The Answer sat in the docket, filed in the grammar. The witness list sat in the docket, chits numbered, the keeper's two receipts beside his name. And now the magistrate gave the hearing its clock.

Tessa read the ruling aloud, in the registry's grammar, in her own hand.

*Each sealed lot shall be read aloud in open session, by the court's clerk, in the order the court files them. Each lot is granted nine minutes of reading. Pages unreached file unread. Unread pages are inadmissible as argument. After each lot, each party is granted nine minutes of reply. The clock is Crown time. The registry's sand is certified.*

Nine minutes. Per lot. Read by the clerk.

The war-marshal set his hand flat on the table. "The magistrate is rationing the hour."

"The magistrate is not judging the hour," Tessa said. "He is *timing* it. The ruling does not say what the lots contain. It says how long the city is permitted to hear them. The seal opens — and the opening has a measure."

She read the second page, and the second page was the weapon.

*The Order shall file, with the lots, its reading order: the pages the clerk shall read within each lot's nine minutes.*

The Order had bought the hour. The Order had sealed it. And now the Order had to choose — on the record, in the file, two days before the hearing — which nine minutes of the King's blackout nights the city would hear read aloud.

"The advocate will spend his nine minutes the way a man spends a knife," Rez said from the door, the blue-white calm gone somewhere colder. "The worst nine. Curated. On the record."

"And the household's nine minutes," the war-marshal said, "are blind."

That was the other edge of the ruling, and the war table sat with it the way men sit with a drawn blade.

The household had never read the sealed lots. The lots were the Order's watching, bought at auction, sealed since the sale. The Order knew what the hour contained. The magistrate knew — the pre-reading, in chambers, sealed, had begun. The household did not. And the ruling granted the household nine minutes of reply per lot, to be spent against a sealed hour the enemy had written, chosen, and ordered.

"Nine minutes," Tessa said, "to answer what we cannot read."

---

The war table spent the day the way the house spent every day now — in the file, in the grammar, in the arithmetic of paper — and the arithmetic was exact.

The household's Answer had made the lots merchandise. The warranty covered the Order's inventory the same as the confession. The magistrate's ruling had not touched the warranty; it had only timed the reading. So the reply could not be about the hour. The hour was sealed, unread, inadmissible except in nine-minute parcels chosen by the enemy.

The reply would be about the envelope.

Tessa wrote the doctrine on the war table's working copy, in her own hand, and the house read it the way it read everything:

*We have never seen the lots. We have seen the lots' receipts. The nine minutes are spent on the receipts.*

Chain of custody. The auction records. The licensed seller, paid, vanished — the ghost with the Crown license, the first hand in the chain. The registry's own warrant of the ghost. The vendor's warranty, *correct as sold*, applied to the Order's sealed stock. The lots were merchandise; the merchandise was warranted; the warranty was the Order's own hand. The hearing would read nine minutes of the King's nights, and the household's nine minutes would read the paper the nights came in — and the docket would own both readings.

"The Order chose the hour," Tessa said. "We choose the envelope. The envelope is the only part of their nine minutes they cannot seal."

"The advocate will read the ghost-seller's license aloud," the war-marshal said, "and the registry will answer that it granted the license. The file owns the ghost."

"Then the nine minutes ask who warranted the ghost," Tessa said. "The registry did. The registry's warranty is in the docket. The magistrate's own clerk granted the license that sealed the lots that will be read in the magistrate's own hearing. That is not an accusation. That is the file's arithmetic." She set the pen down. "The blind reply does not fight the night. It fights the paper the night came in. That is the doctrine."

Zee sat at the head of the table, the tribrid stillness banked underneath, and read the doctrine once.

"File the nine minutes," he said. "Blind."

---

Across the city, in the Order's black, the advocate was choosing his nine.

The file did not record the choosing. The file recorded the filing: at the evening bell, the Order's reading order went into the docket, sealed, with the lots — the pages the clerk would read, nine minutes per lot, in open session, in the court's order. The clerk's hand took it. The registry's sand was certified. Crown time.

And in chambers, sealed, the pre-reading continued — the clerk's eyes moving over pages the household would never see until the hearing read them aloud, the first pages turning in the only room where the hour was allowed to exist unpriced.

The war table knew none of it. The war table knew the arithmetic: nine minutes of the King's nights, chosen by the enemy, read by the court's voice, in two days. The worst nine. Curated. On the record.

"The clerk reads them," Rez said, "not the advocate."

That was the ruling's other mercy, and the house took it the way it took every mercy the file offered — carefully, completely, the way you take a thing that has teeth underneath.

The advocate had stolen the household's cadence to make his corrections read true. The stolen voice would not survive the clerk's mouth. The hearing would hear the King's nights in the court's grammar, read in the court's voice — and the Order's watching, read aloud by the docket, would be the docket's watching, the way the confession had become the docket's confession.

"The Order's weapon," Tessa said, "is read by the enemy's clerk. The lots are the docket's now. The nine minutes are the docket's. The advocate chose the pages, but the voice is the court's." She looked at the sealed docket the way the house looked at everything now. "The seal arms them. The reading muzzles them. That is the magistrate's arithmetic."

---

At the night bell, the morgue-clerk's filing came to the war table, and the paper war got its blood back.

The Crown's incident file for the six dead men had closed its inquest. Six bodies, the Order's black under undyed wool, washed, catalogued, priced — the registry's arithmetic done the way the registry always did it. And the file's last line was the one the war table had been waiting for:

*Unclaimed.*

The Order had been asked for names. The advocate had declined — on the record, in chambers, in the Order's black. Six men in the Order's cloth, and the Order would not claim them, because claiming them admitted the raid, and disavowing them admitted the cloth. So the file owned the silence, and the silence filed as *unclaimed Crown incident*, and the Crown now owned six of the Order's men — washed, priced, filed in the magistrate's own docket, two days from the hearing.

"The registry just claimed the Order's dead as Crown paper," the war-marshal said.

"The Crown's incident file owns six of the Order's men," Tessa said, "and the Order has to either claim them — admitting the raid — or let the Crown keep them, filed, priced, in the docket." She wrote it in her own hand, in the night book's margin, the way the house filed everything. "Exhibit C stands. The silence stands with it. The advocate's nine minutes of reply will have to cover the sealed lots *and* the morgue book. Nine minutes is not much time for six bodies."

Zee signed the night's line the way he signed everything now — his own hand, so no clerk could ever pretend he didn't know.

*The nine minutes are filed. The dead are filed. The paper is ours.*

Two days to the hearing. The lots sat sealed, the reading order sat sealed, the clerk's pre-reading turned its pages in chambers, and the city's clock ran on Crown time — certified sand, nine minutes per lot, read by the court's voice.

Let the hearing read it. The household had never seen the lots, and the household had filed nine minutes anyway — blind, on the receipts, in the grammar — because the paper war was not about the hour.

It was about the envelope. And the envelope was the docket's now.
