# Chapter 226 — The Signature, Ruled

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

Chambers opened Monday with the drowned pages on the clerk's table and the whole courtroom smelling of salt.

They were laid out on canvas, still damp, page against page in the order the sea had returned them — every one of them, the clerk said, the way a man says a prayer he doesn't believe anymore: every one, in order, salt-sealed. The magistrate had ordered them displayed before argument. He wanted the court to look at what it was about to rule on. The last page faced up.

The signature was there. The carrier-name, in a drowned hand, signing its own drowning — the ink bled where the water took it, the letters held where the hand had pressed through. Every letter formed. The flourish cut short by water, not by hesitation.

The clerk's evidence was entered first, because evidence always goes first when nobody trusts the argument.

"The pages," the clerk said, "were returned in the tide's own hand. The sea filed them pre-footnoted — *translation is perjury; the cadence is the testimony* — the same grammar the binders refused to touch." He laid a second paper beside the drowned pages. The guild's refusal, in triplicate. "The binders' guild declines the commission a second time. Binding seals the signature against alteration, and a drowned signature is already altered — by the sea, by the salt, by death. The guild's seal on that binding would make the guild party to an alteration dispute against the ocean."

The magistrate read the refusal twice. Then he looked at the table, and the whole room understood the morning's geometry: the dual record — tide-cadence original sewn alongside the registry translation, line against line — could not be bound until the signature was ruled. And the signature could not be ruled without deciding what a hand is after the water takes it.

The Order's advocate stood.

"A signature," he said, "is an act of will in time. Drowning is the end of time. The hand that died mid-stroke did not sign — it ceased, and the sea finished the stroke. If this court counts a drowned hand as a signatory, then every wreck on the ocean floor is a pending filing, and the registry will be dredging litigants until the tide-tables run out."

He laid one paper on the clerk's table. Not triplicate. He had learned something about triplicate.

"The refusal enters the record unsigned. The magistrate's dual-record order says it must be filed anyway — the file's own perjury, signed by the order and un-signable. The drowned signature changes nothing. A dead hand is no hand. The refusal dies on the seal line."

The King's counsel stood. He answered the way he always answers now — sitting down, the way he had in the gap ruling — because he had triplicate and the advocate had one paper.

"Binding law," the King's counsel said, "requires intent, act, and seal. The intent was formed before the water. The act was completed in the water. The sea sealed it with salt. The hand did not die mid-signature. It died mid-breath. The signature is whole."

He turned the last page toward the magistrate. "Look at the blood."

The courtroom went still. I had seen it before the argument — the clerk had shown me in chambers, the way the clerk shows me everything, the way a man tells a priest. Where the drowned hand had cut itself on the barnacle edge of the wreck-page, the blood had met the salt at the margin, and the salt had sealed it there — blood and ink and brine, one seal, made by the water and the wound together. The signature was not written in ink alone. It was written in the only medium the drowning had left.

"The seal," the King's counsel said, "is the sea's own. The sea sealed the signature it carried. A sealed signature is a bound signature."

The magistrate looked at me. Not at the counsel. At me. I was not a party. I was standing by the exits the way I always do. He looked at me because he knew what I am — the King's arithmetic, standing where the exits are, pricing things.

I said nothing. The floor was not mine.

The magistrate ruled.

He ruled the drowned hand a signatory — the carrier-name had signed, and the signature held. But he ruled the second thing, the thing I had been pricing since the pages came up from the water, and it was not the ruling either side had asked for.

The magistrate created the category. A drowned signature, he ruled, is a **sealed signatory**: bound to the file, present on the record, and procedurally unreachable. The court could bind the hand. The court could never serve it. The carrier-name was now a litigant the file owned and could not summon, a signature the court held and could not enforce against, a party that existed on the record exactly the way the drowned exist in the sea — present, and beyond reach.

"The court," the magistrate said, "binds the hand it cannot summon. The file keeps the signature. The signature keeps the silence."

The dread went through the room the way dread goes through rooms now — not as surprise, as arithmetic. The Order had wanted the signature dead so the refusal would die with it. The King had wanted the signature live so the record would stand. The magistrate had given them both a signature that was neither: alive enough to bind the file, dead enough to never answer it.

And then the magistrate ruled the third thing. The binding.

"The dual record," he said, "is ordered bound under the court's seal."

The clerk did not move. The guild's refusal was already on the table, in triplicate. The magistrate read it a third time, and the room watched him do the arithmetic I had already done in chambers: he could hold the guild in contempt. Contempt requires naming the defiance. Naming the defiance means declaring the sea's edit an alteration — and the alteration question is the whole case. The file could not punish the binders without first ruling the ruling it was afraid to make.

The magistrate set the refusal down. He did not rule contempt. The dual record — tide-cadence original, registry translation, line against line, the war's most-cited exhibit — sat on the clerk's table, written, cited, reconciled, ruled on, and un-bindable. Because the last hand in the chain was a craftsman's, and the craftsman had read the pages and declined the honor, twice.

The King came to the table after chambers. He read the altered clause himself — the carrier clause, read against the registry's copy, line against line, in the open record the binders would not seal. The terms differed. The carrier-name had signed a transaction whose carrier clause had been re-written in a hand that was not the carrier's. The alteration was visible, on the page, under his eyes. Its author was not.

He read it twice. The heat came off him without his calling it — the damp pages steamed where his shadow fell, and the clerk's ink curled on the vellum nearest his hand. He did not name the fire. The fire has no name in this file.

"Then," the King said, "the file needs the author."

He left the pages on the table. The clerk covered them with the canvas. The courtroom kept the smell of salt.

The file had priced the drowning. It had priced the signature. It had priced the sea itself, summoned and cross-examinable, wearing a litigant's standing like a borrowed coat.

It had no price for an author.

That price was the King's to name. And he had not named it yet.

The file keeps what the file keeps. The file keeps the drowned hand now. Sealed. Unsummonable. Signed.
