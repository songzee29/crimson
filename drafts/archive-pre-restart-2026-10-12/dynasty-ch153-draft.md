# Chapter 153 — "Drovak Senn"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

Drovak Senn chose a church.

That was the last thing he chose, and Zee respected the arithmetic of it even as he walked the drowned-blocks at midnight, rain threading the gutters, and thought *seven*. Six buried. Tonight, seven.

The chapel was small and salt-stained, a parish house where the faithful came to pray for sailors who didn't come home. Sanctuary there was older than kings — older than the idea of kings — and the priest who met Zee on the steps had the face of a man who had already decided the thing he was about to be asked.

"He's inside," the priest said. "He confessed. The sanctuary was given."

"Then you know who I am."

"I know who you are." The priest's hand rested on the chapel door, steady as stone. "I also know what he is becoming. Come and see."

---

Inside, the candles were guttering in no wind.

Senn knelt at the rail with his head down, and he was praying in two voices — one his own, thin and terrified, and one underneath it that was not a voice at all but a *grinding*, wet, wrong. The marrow was working in him. Zee could smell it: the Demon rot the Order of the Hollow Crown had poured into a wineglass vial and handed to a desperate man in a chapel, the same unmaking he'd once pulled out of Elias Marr's blood with his own two hands.

*They learned it from me,* Zee thought, and the horror of the night landed cold behind his ribs. Every mercy he'd ever worked became a lesson in the Order's hands. He'd stabilized Elias into one race, and they'd studied the stabilization, and now they'd built it faster and crueler and sold it to a man hiding from a king.

The grinding voice rose. Senn's spine arched like a drawn bow. His fingers had too many joints.

The priest didn't look at Zee. He looked at the man at the rail — at what was left of him — and put his shoulder to the inner door and opened it himself, and stepped aside.

He broke the sanctuary from the inside. Because the thing on the rail wasn't a man seeking mercy anymore. It was the Order's experiment wearing a man's confession, and the oldest law in the city had nothing left to protect.

"Thank you," Zee said quietly.

"Don't," the priest said.

---

Zee crossed the chapel floor and the guttering candles bowed, one by one, the way the fire in Jorren Vale's study had bowed — lesser flames acknowledging a greater. Senn's head came up. His eyes were wrong now, one his own, one something else's, and the man's own eye was the one that wept.

"King," he said, in the thin voice, the true one. "Please."

Zee had been asked for mercy seven times in as many weeks, in seven different rooms, and every one of them had meant something different. Thoss had meant *be quick*. Vane had meant *let me choose the room*. Sera had meant *prove you were listening*. Vale had meant *nothing at all — he was already gone*.

Senn meant *don't let them have me*. Not the death. The *making*.

The fire came off Zee's shoulders in wings of shadow and burning light, and the chapel's old stone drank the color of it, and Zee put his hand on Drovak Senn's chest the way he'd put it on Vale's — and this time it wasn't the hunger that rose. This time it was something older and cleaner and worse: a king doing the one thing his whole nature had ever been built for, and doing it *kindly*, because the Order's unmaking was a slower death than any fire he carried.

Senn's true eye cleared for one breath at the end. One breath, one look, one *thank you* —

— and the bell in the chapel tower struck once, deep, though no hand had touched the rope, and Drovak Senn was gone the way men go in stories told to frighten children: all at once, and completely, and without anything left to bury.

Only the vial remained, unbroken on the rail. Empty. The Order's handwriting on the glass.

Zee picked it up and crushed it in his fist, and his blood — crimson-black, steaming — took the glass dust and the last of the marrow both, and the embers breathed, brighter than they'd ever been, and said nothing at all.

---

The priest anointed Zee's hands with oil from the sanctuary lamp.

It was an old rite — for the dying, for the grieving, for men coming home from wars they didn't choose. The priest's thumb moved over Zee's knuckles, slow, and the oil shone in the candlelight, and the priest said, low, "I'll ask my goddess to forgive the king."

Zee looked at him for a long moment. The crimson-black horns caught the guttering light. The scales had come up his throat like a tide of dark glass.

"Don't," Zee said.

He walked out of the chapel into the rain, and the city opened for him the way it always did, and behind him the bell struck the hour wrong — one hour off, and it would stay one hour off, the parish would say, for as long as the stones remembered.

*Seven,* he thought.

*Selk Varren. Brannic Dor. Vaeren Thoss. Kaldrek Vane. Sera Quill. Jorren Vale. Drovak Senn.*

*Seven.*

The list had eight names. The eighth wasn't a kill. It was a debt — a frightened accountant carrying ledgers to House Vesper, alive because he'd held a pen and never a knife.

Zee walked home through the rain with ash on his coat and the vial's dust in his blood, and the hunger coiled down, sated and patient, and underneath it the embers breathed — in and out, in and out — and the window that was off its hinges stood open on the night, and somewhere in the dark his father watched.

*Let's see what you do with the list finished, my son.*
