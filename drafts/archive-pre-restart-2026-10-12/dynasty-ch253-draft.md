# Chapter 253 — "The Books, Burned"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The choirbooks were beautiful. That was the worst of it.

Ner found them at the seventh farm — bound in the farm's own leather, copied out in three hands, the new verse written true in registry grammar, the harmony annotated in the margins the way the gate school had once annotated its own curriculum. The house had bought the nursery curriculum back to unmake it; the farms had written their own. The dread came up through Ner's throat like a swallowed chord, because the fathers had been good scholars. They had learned the true thing faithfully, the way the well had taught it, and now he was going to stand in their kitchen and tell them their good scholarship would get their children filed.

He set the books on the kitchen table, one by one. Seven farms. Eleven books. The ink was barely dry on three of them.

"This is the record," he said to the kitchen full of fathers, "and a record is the thing the file reads. So there will be no record."

The farmer from the third well — the broad man with salt under his nails, the first mouth Ner had taught — stood up and said, "We wrote it so the children would learn it right."

"They don't get to learn it right," Ner said, and his voice was the warmest, worst thing in the room. "Right is what gets you kept. I'm here to make you all wrong."

He burned them in the yard. All eleven. The leather curled and the pages went up in smoke that smelled like church, and the fathers stood around the fire with their hands at their sides and their mouths moving in the wrong cadence, humming it, humming the ruin, and the smoke went up into the dark over the inland farms and nobody wrote down what it was.

The land remembers the lie even after the books are ash. That was the point. The books had been the mouth's crutch. Without them, the fathers had to hum it from the gut, and the gut kept it wrong.

---

Then Ner taught them the breath.

One inhale per missing beat. That was the whole technique, and it was the cruelest thing he had ever taught, because it meant the wells — the *water* — had to learn to forget. He took the fathers down to the seventh well at dusk and stood at the stone edge and did it himself, first: sang the verse wrong, and on every missing beat, where the true note should have landed, he drew one long deliberate breath. In. Hold. Let the true note die unmade. Sing the next wrong one.

"When they come," he said, "you don't sing the truth and you don't refuse. You sing wrong and you breathe through the true. The well will follow the breath. It's how I taught it at the third."

The well followed the breath. The chord underneath the verse — the thing that had been humming since before the farm had a name — took the inhale and let the true note go, and the water went wrong in the dark, and five fathers breathed in the rhythm of their own land forgetting.

Ner sat at the edge with his feet over the dark and he thought of his hand over a small boy's mouth in another dark, a lifetime ago, and he thought: *the keeping graduated, and I am the one who graduated it, and there is no floor above this one.*

---

The inspector came at dawn.

She had the form. *Instruction, of rendition, inland.* She had the chalk for the lintel and the wax for the seal and two men with registry staves behind her, and she went down to the seventh well with the fathers lined up behind her, and she told them to draw, and she told the well to sing.

The well sang.

It sang in Ner's cadence. Wrong notes, rotted chord, the missing beats breathed through, one long inhale where the true verse should have been. The fathers drew the water with their mouths moving in the ruin, quiet, obedient, wrong on purpose, and the inspector stood at the stone edge with her form in her hand and listened to the land fail to render.

She wrote: *rendition, satisfactory.*

Then she turned, and her eyes went narrow the way registry eyes went narrow, and she said, "Satisfactory is a word for this season. The registry will re-inspect when the water turns."

And one of the fathers — the third-well farmer, the first mouth, the broad man with salt under his nails — said, because he was brave and stupid and a father, "The water won't turn, lady. It turned."

The inspector's hand went to her wax. Not the chalk. The wax — the seal-stick, the thing she used for exceptions, for *findings*.

Ner was across the yard before the thought finished.

He did not touch her. He didn't have to. The road behind her was the northern road, and the northern road was soft, and at the end of it stood the King, because Zee had said *you mark it, you come home and you tell me*, and Ner had marked this one three farms back with the wrong cadence taught too well, and the inspector had heard the satisfaction and still reached for the wax.

Zee walked into the yard the way a storm walks into a harbor — unhurried, structural, the scales finishing their crawl up his neck, dark glass set with the decision — and the registry staves went up and came down again empty, because Zee didn't fight the staves. He went around them. His hand closed on the inspector's wrist before the wax hit the paper, and the wrist broke like a dry twig, and the scream went up into the morning over the farms.

"Don't," Zee said, quiet, the way he got when the violence was in the paper — but the paper was here now, in the yard, in the morning, and it had a wrist, and it was reaching.

She went for the finding with her other hand — brave, stupid, loyal, the way registry people were — and Zee's other hand took her throat, and the Wolf in him and the Vampire in him and the Witch in him moved at once, and the Dragon blood moved in him the way it moved when the danger was structural, and beneath all of it the burning that had no name stayed banked, stayed patient. He didn't need it. This was a throat morning. This was what throats were for.

He broke her neck clean and set her down in the yard the way you set down a finished instrument, and the two men with the staves were on their knees before he finished turning, and he said to them, "You inspected. It was satisfactory. You file it."

They filed it.

The fathers watched. The well hummed. The smoke from the choirbooks was gone from the air, but the smell was still in Ner's clothes, and he stood in the yard with the warmest, worst dread of his whole life, because the inspector had been marked and the King had closed the mouth and no record would ever say what happened at the seventh farm — and that was the whole point, and that was also the whole cost.

Zee came to him and put the hand at the back of his neck, blood and all, and said, "You're the wrong they learn. And I'm the reason they don't get quoted."

Ner leaned into the hand, and the fathers breathed, and the land forgot, and the keeping held — inland, in Ner's cadence, with no record.
