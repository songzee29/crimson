# Chapter 224 — The Gap, Ruled

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

The magistrate opened chambers on Friday with the King's filing on top of the stack and the whole brokers' hall holding its breath underneath it.

He had read the triplicate the night before. The clerk had told me — the clerk tells me everything the way a man tells a priest, because the clerk has stopped believing in magistrates and started believing in ink. The magistrate read it at his desk, in chambers, alone, and did not finish his wine. That was the tell. Magistrates finish their wine.

The King claimed the nine minutes as Crown property. The Order's own certification, quoted back, page and line. The advocate's own signature. The stamp.

The magistrate's ruling was the only thing standing between the King and the gap. And the magistrate was standing in the gap himself, because his own certification had deeded it.

Chambers opened. The advocate stood. The King's counsel stood. I stood — I stand for rulings now, the way I stand for settlements; I have learned that sitting is what you do when you trust the floor, and I do not trust the floor.

The advocate went first, because the advocate always goes first — he has the most to lose and the oldest paper, and those are the two things chambers respects.

"The King's filing," he said, "claims the nine minutes. The claim rests on our certification. We do not dispute the certification. We dispute the arithmetic." He laid a paper on the clerk's table. One paper. Not triplicate. He had learned something about triplicate since yesterday.

"The magistrate certified the Order's tide-table nine minutes wrong. The certification deeded the defect. The King claims the deed. But the defect is the defect in the *Order's* table — the gap between the Crown's clock and ours. The Crown's clock was never wrong. The gap belongs to the party whose table the gap is in."

The King's counsel answered without standing up — I watched him do it; it was the coldest gesture in the room. He answered sitting down, because he had triplicate and the advocate had one paper.

"The certification," the King's counsel said, "is Crown paper. It was filed by this court, under this court's seal, against Crown time. The defect was deeded by the Crown. The Crown's deed makes the gap Crown property, whatever table the gap was found in. The Order cannot deed a thing and then claim the deed belongs to the drowner."

The magistrate looked at me. Not at the counsel. At me. I was not a party. I was standing by the exits the way I always do. He looked at me because he knew what I am — the King's arithmetic, standing where the exits are, pricing things.

I said nothing. The floor was not mine.

The magistrate ruled.

He ruled the gap Crown property — the King's nine minutes, deeded by the court's own certification, held against the docket's balance, administered under the court's seal. The Order's certification was the deed, and the deed ran to the Crown. The brokers' market, he ruled, was trading in Crown property without a license, and every gap-filing executed since yesterday morning was a filing on Crown time.

And then he ruled the second thing. The thing I had been pricing since the evening bell.

"The gap," the magistrate said, "is priced by its holder. The Crown holds it. The Crown prices it."

The price was read into the record. The Crown's price for gap-filing, per tide-turn, filed by the registry at the magistrate's order, payable to the Crown's clerk, in the Crown's hand:

The King could not close the gap without un-filing the defect that kept the docket from balancing. The King knew it. The magistrate knew it. The file knew it — the certification was Crown paper, and un-filing Crown paper voids every filing that cited it, and the defect had been cited in forty-one filings across three dockets, including the sea's. So the gap stayed open. The gap had to stay open. The King now owned nine minutes per tide-turn of time that could never be closed, and the only way to keep it from being the enemy's market was to keep it himself — at Crown expense, in perpetuity, administered by a registry office that had to be built from nothing because no existing office could file in time the Crown owned but never ran.

The cost: the registry's new office, staffed and salaried. The gap ledger, kept by clerks who filed in unowned time and stopped sleeping. The revenue: the League's rent, the brokers' license fees, the price per tide-turn, paid into the Crown's hand.

And the dread — the arithmetic that made the whole room go quiet the second time — was the net.

The League's rent was priced against the cost of keeping the gap. The brokers' licenses were priced against the rent. The price per tide-turn was priced against the licenses. And at the bottom of the column, where the clerk's pen always ends up, was the one number the file could never un-file:

The King was paying himself rent on his own nine minutes, to keep the defect certified, to keep the docket balanced, and the market — the beautiful, terrible, honest market — was the mechanism that made the defect permanent.

The Order had leased the gap for one afternoon. The King would lease it forever. The enemy had sublet the difference for a day's profit; the King bought the whole building, and the building had to stay broken, because the building's brokenness was what kept the Crown's docket from falling apart.

The advocate understood it first. I saw his face change — not the flinch of a man surprised by a ruling; the stillness of a man who has just watched his opponent buy the ruin at full price and realized the ruin is worth more broken than whole. He bowed to the magistrate. He sat down. The Order had lost the gap and won the arithmetic, because the King now had to hold the gap open forever, and forever is the one instrument the Order never has to pay rent on.

The ruling was filed. The clerk entered it. The gap was Crown property. The gap was priced by its holder.

The holder was the King.

The price was the defect, held open, on file, in perpetuity — paid for by the man who had been clever enough to claim it.

The chamber emptied. The brokers went back to the brokers' hall and reopened the market with the Crown's license on the door. The League's broker paid the first rent in the Crown's hand and filed his next contract in the gap, on Crown time, sealed with the magistrate's seal, legal now — the same kind of contract, the same kind of sealed delivery, the same kind of canvas-wrapped silence, but licensed.

The King owned the gap. The King priced the gap. And the gap — the nine minutes the enemy's table had gotten wrong, certified by the court's own hand — would stay open as long as the docket needed it.

Which was forever. That was the ruling. That was the price. That was who paid it.

The file keeps what the file keeps. The file keeps the defect now. In triplicate.
