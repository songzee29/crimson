# Chapter 229 — "The Sea-Side Window"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

Dawn came up over the water the way it always did — gold first, then the rest of the world — and the boys were already at the sea-side window.

Ner had expected to be the one to break the King's order first. He hadn't expected to lose the race to a ten-year-old and a six-year-old.

Zayrlen stood with his forehead against the glass, his breath fogging it and clearing it and fogging it again, the way he'd done since he was small enough to be carried to the sill. Azren sat cross-legged on the window seat with his chin on his knees, watching the shallows. The shutters Zee had ordered the night before stood open. Somebody — Ner knew somebody, the way mothers know which hands moved in the night — had unlatched them before dawn and folded them back like the night order had been a suggestion.

"The King said the windows stay shut," Ner said from the doorway.

Zayrlen didn't turn. His crimson-gold eyes tracked the foam-lines below. "The King didn't say the windows stay shut, Baba. He said the shutters stay shut." A pause, ten years old and already the file's own grammar in his mouth. "The window isn't a shutter. The window is where you look at what you shut."

Ner came into the room. He didn't re-latch the shutters. He stood the watch instead — by day now, in the doorway, the way he'd stood it by night at the basin. The house got its day guard the way it got its night guard: unfiled.

Below the house, the shallows did the thing the basin had done in the dark. Two foam-lines drew themselves along the sand, parallel, and redrew themselves, and redrew themselves again — the first tide's line and the second tide's, arguing in public, in foam, in daylight, where anyone standing at a window could read them. The sea had taken the King's shutter order and answered it by filing its dispute in the open.

Azren lifted his finger to the condensation on the glass and started drawing.

Ner felt his throat close the way it had closed when he found the door on the scrap's back — the gold-light handle, drawn steady, by a hand too young for handles. The infant wasn't archiving anymore. He was *mapping*. And now the youngest son was mapping the argument itself: two foam-lines, in gold shimmer, curving across the window's glass — and at the place where the two lines crossed, a door. With a handle.

"He's not drawing what he sees," Zayrlen said. He was the older brother; the reading came to him first, the way the reading always came to him first. "He's drawing what the argument's *about*."

Ner put his hand on the back of Azren's head. Gold on gold. The boy didn't look up. "What is it about, little one?"

"The place they both walk around," Azren said.

That was the exact shape Ner had heard in the basin in the dark — the drowned thing the two cadences circled, the record the second tide had exposed by arriving, turning between the tides like a door. His six-year-old had drawn in condensation what the King couldn't kill and the file couldn't file. Ner breathed fog onto the glass beside it and let the drawing stay. He had folded the first door back into the drawer. He wasn't folding this one anywhere. Some keeping had to stay by hand.

Then the attention changed, and the whole room felt it.

Rin was in the doorway. Barefoot, the way he was always barefoot, the way boys with no names and no filings stand in doorways — and the doubled water in the shallows below had turned to look at him.

Not at the window. Not at the boys. At *Rin*.

Three nights ago Rin had asked the water for his mother, and the chamber had gone still and attentive, and Ner had stood in the doorway and let the ocean take an interest. He hadn't known then that the attention had a second half — that the sea that listened had a twin that answered. The first tide had heard the boy. The second tide had arrived. And now the second tide was answering him.

The foam-lines in the shallows bent. Both of them. Toward the house. Toward the window. Toward the boy nobody had priced.

Ner moved. He was across the room before the dread finished forming, and he put his body between Rin and the window — not the glass, the *boy*, the way you stand between a child and weather — and he pulled Rin back by hand, into the room, away from the glass, the way the house kept everything that mattered now: by hand, in the dark, with no record.

"You're not the subject," Ner said. He said it to Rin. He meant it for the sea.

Rin's eyes were huge. "It heard me, didn't it."

"It hears everything at the waterline," Ner said. "That's not the same as being heard."

He was lying to the boy the way fathers lie — the good lie, the one that costs the liar — because the truth was that the water had been *attentive*, and attentive was the one thing the sea had never been to Rin before, and the sea didn't adopt, it annexed, and the one child the house had promised nobody would price was standing in a doorway being *noticed* by an ocean that had learned his name from his own question.

Zayrlen turned from the glass. His crimson-gold eyes went from Ner to Rin and back, and Ner saw his eldest-of-the-two doing the arithmetic the house did now — counting by hand. "Baba," Zayrlen said quietly, "the second line stopped."

Ner looked past Rin's head. In the shallows, the first foam-line went on drawing and redrawing itself. The second was gone. Not faded — *withdrawn*. The argument had paused, the way arguments pause when someone walks into the room. The second tide had turned from the sea to the window, and having seen what it came to see, it had left the shallows to the first tide alone.

The attention had its answer. The boy who asked was the subject after all — or the sea had decided he was, which was the same thing, because the sea didn't file disputes.

"Baba," Azren said from the window seat, very small. "The door's handle turned."

Ner looked. On the condensation, at the crossing of the two gold-light foam-lines, the drawn door stood — and its handle, drawn steady in gold shimmer by a hand too young for handles, had rotated. A quarter-turn. The way a handle turns when somebody on the other side tries it.

Ner breathed fog onto the glass over the drawing. He watched the gold-light lines blur and run. The keeping went back to being by hand.

"Breakfast," he said. His voice didn't shake. It had learned not to, the way his hands had learned. "All of you. Now."

They went. Rin went last, and at the doorway he stopped and looked back at the window, at the sea that had heard him and the second sea that had answered. Ner put his hand on the back of the boy's neck — gold warmth, the marriage's other ledger, the keeping the house had sworn — and steered him gently toward the stairs.

The King's shutters stood open. The sea's answer stood in them. And Ner understood, the way fathers understand things they don't say aloud, that Zee could shut the house against the water and he could stand every watch there was, and it wouldn't matter — because the boy had asked the question himself, and the asking was the one door the house had never learned to keep.
