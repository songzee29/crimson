# Chapter 252 — "The Keeping, Inland"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The decision took Ner four days, and he made it the way he made all his hard ones — with his hands busy, because his hands had never once asked for a reason.

He mended the gate-school boots. He re-strung the laundry lines. He sat in the missing hour with his hand over nobody's mouth, because the boy was old enough to keep his own tongue now, and the hand hung in the air above the bed like an instrument the music had left. On the fourth day he set the boot down, and he said it to the kitchen, to the steam off the kettle, to nobody, because the house was built on telling things to nobody and trusting the walls to keep them.

"The waterline got bigger than my keeping."

The walls kept it.

Nymeris had warned him for free — get him inland, while the water still calls it a courtesy — and Ner had obeyed it, and the obedience had been a receipt, not directions, because when he got there the farmers' mouths were already humming the new verse in registry grammar, low, in the kitchens, around the wells, the way a coast hums in the morning before the tide. The land answering the sea in its own tongue. Nobody had taught them. That was the whole of the terror. The keeping had been his hands, his mouth, his songs wrong on purpose in the gate school, and now the earth itself was singing in the enemy's cadence and doing it *right*, and if the well inland could learn the true verse on its own, the well inland could teach it — to the farms, to the inspectors, to the file.

He went to Zee at the ninth bell and said, "I have to go inland."

Zee looked at him the way he looked at him when the room was about to lose a man for a week — the scales starting their dark crawl up his neck, the decision spreading with them. "You're asking me if the farms are the house's to silence."

"No," Ner said. "I already know they're not. I'm asking you to hold the door while I teach them to be wrong instead."

Zee's hand came up and settled at the back of Ner's neck — the warm, owned weight of it, the hand that had broken kings — and rested there the whole of one long breath, and then he said, "Take the northern road. It's softest. And if a mouth opens that I need to close, you don't touch it. You mark it. You come home and you tell me."

"And if it's me they need," Ner said, "then I'm the wrong they learn."

"Then you're the wrong they learn."

---

The first farm was the one at the third well, and the well was humming before he crested the rise — a low wet chord under the morning, the new verse, true as a struck bell, in registry grammar, the grammar the Order's nursery inspectors had filed under *instruction, of rendition, inland*. Ner stood on the road and listened to the water sing itself to the file, and the sound went through him like a hook, because he had been teaching wrong notes for a year and the earth had skipped straight past him to the source.

The farmer was a broad man with salt under his nails, and his mouth was moving while he worked the pump.

Ner said, "I need your kitchen. And your men. And you need to stop humming that."

The farmer's mouth stopped. His hands didn't. "The inspector has a form for this," he said. "Instruction, of rendition, inland. We signed it. The well teaches. We learn."

"I know," Ner said. "I'm here to teach it worse."

---

By mouth. In the dark. In farm kitchens where the inspector had already chalked the form number on the lintel.

That was how Ner had done it at the gate school, and that was how he did it now — the ruined cadence, the deliberate corruption, note by note, mouth by mouth. He sat at the third well's table with five farmers and the farmer's wife and taught them the verse wrong the way he had once taught a hallway of children to sing a song wrong on purpose, and the difference was that the children had been his joy and this was his grief, because every man at this table was somebody's father and he was un-teaching fathers the one true thing the land knew.

He taught them the missing beat. He taught them where the breath went wrong. He taught them the lullaby the way the enemy would never file it, because the enemy only filed true things.

"You hum it like this when they come," he said, and his voice did the thing, the golden wrong of it, and five throats followed him into the ruin. "And you don't write it. There is no paper for this. If I find paper, I burn it."

The farmer's wife was crying, quiet, the way country women cried, and she said, "They'll know. They inspect."

"They'll know the well," Ner said, "and the well is learning too. I just have to get to it first."

---

The well was stone and older than the farm, and it held the chord the way a throat holds a swallow.

Ner went down to it alone at dusk with his lantern, and the verse rose up out of the dark water, true and wet and perfect, the sea's grammar in the registry's mouth — the thing Nymeris had once warned him about and then sold him as a courtesy — and he did the only thing he knew how to do with a true thing that wanted to kill his house.

He sang it wrong at it.

One verse. Two. The wrong notes dropped into the dark like stones, and the well took them, and the water shifted, and the chord underneath the verse — the chord that had been humming since before Ner was born — began, note by note, to rot. Deliberate. Corrupted. His cadence, sung into the water, by a father who had learned the technique on his own son's mouth, one night at a time, in the dark.

The wrong went down like dye. The water hummed it back at him, wrong now, his.

Ner sat at the edge with his feet over the dark and his face wet — not from the well — and whispered to nobody, "That's the whole beat, then. The keeping graduated from the child's tongue to the land's."

The well hummed the ruin. In Ner's cadence. With no record.

He got up and walked the night road to the second farm, and his boots were wet, and the night was cold, and the land was learning to sing wrong on purpose, and he kept teaching anyway.
