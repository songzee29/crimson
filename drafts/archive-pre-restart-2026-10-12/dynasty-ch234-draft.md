# Chapter 234 — "The Boys Speak the Sea"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

Ner found them at the third hour, which was the hour the house had quietly stopped filing.

The nursery's anteroom was dark except for the coals in the grate and the seam of salt-light under the sea-side window, and Zayrlen's pallet lay where it had lain for nine nights — by the door, folded blanket, the boy's body a long line under it, ten years old and sleeping the way soldiers sleep: with one ear on the room.

Azren wasn't asleep. Azren was laughing in his bed, soft, the way he had laughed every night for a month — not a child's laughter. The tide's cadence, worn like a child's mouth wears it. The laughing that stopped when the eldest was there.

Ner stood in the corridor and didn't go in. Because the laughing had stopped.

What was coming through the door's seam instead was *conversation*.

Zayrlen's voice — low, awake, the ten-year-old's voice with the sleep scraped off it — moving in a grammar Ner had heard in only three places: the basin at low tide, the shore-folk's mouths when they weren't performing for the Crown, and his own son's throat when the water was speaking through it.

Answering. Fluent. Unhurried. The eldest son of the house, answering the youngest in the sea's grammar, learned the way children learn everything the schools refuse to name — by being near it, by hearing it, by living in the same room as the mouth that had it first.

Ner's hand found the doorframe. The gold went dim under his skin the way it went when he was holding something too heavy to set down in a corridor.

He had taught Azren the second verse. In land-words. In the house's own tongue. The one the tide never learned — the one he had sung inland, on purpose, in the dark, believing that what he taught by hand, in the dark, the water couldn't have.

Then he had held his own hand over his son's mouth, every night, until the tide-cadence humming stopped — the keeping done by hand in the dark, the way the house did everything now. And Azren, six years old, gold shimmer and sea-light, had learned to hum through his nose, soft and steady, the water's grammar slipping past his father's hand the way water slips past a dam.

The father had tried to ruin the words by hand in the dark. Twice he had tried — once by singing around them, once by stopping them.

And the eldest had learned them anyway. Not from Ner. From his brother's mouth.

That was the arithmetic that sat Ner down in the corridor with his back against the doorframe, because there was no filing for it and no lesson for it: the teaching had changed hands twice, and both times it had walked around him. Ner had taught the song. Ner had stopped the song. And the song had gone from Azren's mouth to Zayrlen's ear the way brothers trade everything — untaught, uncounted, unfiled.

Inside, Zayrlen finished whatever he was saying, and Azren answered in the same cadence — a small, delighted string of it, the gold shimmer in the dark — and the two of them laughed. Not the tide's laughter now. Brothers' laughter. The room sounded like a nursery again, for nine seconds, and Ner sat in the corridor with his hands over his face and understood the warmest, most terrible thing the house had taught him yet.

The boys were fluent now. He was the only one who couldn't read them.

He had carried them both. He had birthed them both — Zayrlen first, then Azren, separate births, the gold blood doing its work the way it had always done, naturally, without a surrogate and without a bargain. His blood ran in both of them. And the sea had taken the blood he gave them and made a language out of it, and taught it to his sons, and left the father standing in a corridor listening to his own children speak a grammar he could not parse — fluent, easy, brothers.

The house couldn't keep a mouth it didn't own. That was the line, and it was the whole beat, and it was warm as bread and sharp as a hook.

He heard Zayrlen's voice again — land-words this time, the boy's own, bright and ten: "Say it back slower, little tide. Baba's out there. He'll want to hear the fast part tomorrow."

Ner stood. He didn't wipe his face. He went in.

Azren sat up in his bed, gold shimmer, grinning the gap-toothed grin of a six-year-old who has just been praised by his hero. Zayrlen sat up on his pallet, crimson-gold eyes catching the coals, and smiled the way the heir smiled when he was caught doing something the file would have priced.

"Tell me the fast part tomorrow," Ner said. It cost him everything and it showed in nothing. That was the keeping. That was the whole of it.

---

The blood came later that same night, and it came the way everything in this house came now: by hand, in the dark, with no record.

Azren's humming started at the fourth hour. Ner was at the bed before it reached the window. His hand over his son's mouth — the keeping, the charge, the form the registry's lullaby inspectors had a name for. *Withholding, of a rendition due.*

Azren went still under his hand. Then the gold shimmer flared, and the boy's teeth closed.

Blood welled between Ner's fingers, warm, immediate. The boy held. Ner didn't pull away.

He held until the humming stopped — not through the teeth, through the nose, the sea's grammar going thin and then gone — and then he lifted his hand and looked at his son. Azren's eyes were six-year-old eyes again, wet, afraid, the tide gone out of them like water off sand.

"It's all right," Ner said, and it wasn't, and they both knew it wasn't, and it was still the truest thing in the room. "It's all right, little tide. Sleep."

He wrapped his hand in his own sleeve. Zayrlen stood in the anteroom doorway, crimson-gold eyes on the blood, and Ner saw his eldest-of-the-two doing the arithmetic the house did now — counting by hand.

"Does the file get that one?" Zayrlen asked quietly.

"No," Ner said. "Some keeping doesn't get filed."

Zayrlen nodded. He went back to his pallet by the door. The laughing stopped. That was the whole defense, and it had never once been written down.

Ner sat in the chair by the bed until the house went quiet, and the gold under his skin dimmed, and the last thing he thought before sleep took him was that he had tried to ruin the words by hand in the dark, and his sons were speaking them anyway, in the dark, unfiled — and that the sea had never needed his permission. It had only ever needed his blood, and he had given it that freely, in love, in his own body, twice.

His hands were wet when he woke. He didn't remember answering anything.

Azren was laughing in his sleep. The tide's cadence. Happy.

The laughing stopped when Zayrlen sat up.
