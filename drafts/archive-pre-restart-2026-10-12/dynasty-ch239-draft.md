# Chapter 239 — The Arithmetic, Turning

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

The registry had forged the evidence, the registry had filed the forgery, and the registry now had to adjudicate its own hand — and the first thing the registry did, because registries always do this first, was reach for the rule that would make the problem someone else's.

The magistrate ruled it at the third low tide, standing in the water between the courts with the intake journal open in the clerk's hands.

"The correction stands," he said. "The original stands beside it."

Two names for one debt. The corrected carrier-name — the one the file could carry, bearer, standing, citable, the face the Order bought at salvage and carried on its books at full value. And the barnacled original — the tide-cadence name, the one the sea carried for what it took, readable by exactly one hand in the kingdom.

"A name in dispute," the magistrate said, "requires a guardian. The registry will seat one."

That was the arithmetic turning. I will write it plain because it deserves plain: the registry's own grammar had just been caught forging a record, and the registry's solution — procedural, total, airtight — was to make the one man neither side owned legally responsible for carrying it.

The guardian would be the disowned advocate. The hand that swore to the waterline. The counsel the Order fired, the sea retained, and the registry could not fire because the registry had not hired him — and now, by the magistrate's ruling, the registry was hiring him anyway, as guardian of the carrier-name, legally required to be present wherever either version of the name was filed, in either court, at every low tide.

The Order moved within the hour. Not a motion — a claim of ownership.

The Order's counsel filed it standing in the corridor with the intake journal's seal still wet: *the correction is the registry's paper, but the receipt is ours. We bought the receipt at salvage, in the registry's hand, filed, numbered, proper. The correction rides inside the receipt. What we bought, we own — forgery included. The corrected name is the Order's property, and the registry's grammar of disputed names does not reach into property held at salvage.*

Accession. What you buy, you own, even the rot inside the walls. The Order's coldest arithmetic yet: it had bought the registry's forgery as genuine evidence, and now that the forgery was exposed, it claimed the forgery as its own asset — the correction as property, the corrected name as a holding, the face value it had carried on its books as its private fiction, untouchable by the magistrate's guardianship.

If the magistrate granted it, the disowned advocate would be guardian of a name the Order owned. He would carry the corrected name in the Order's hand, at the Order's tolerance, in the enemy's drawer — and the Order would hold the only lever that could release him.

I priced it for the King's counsel that night: the Order was not defending the receipt. It was *adopting the forgery*. It wanted the correction licensed, owned, held — because a licensed forgery is a fact, and facts are what the Order builds with.

The King summoned the advocate. Not to the war room — to the small chamber off the registry, the one with no windows, where the King prices things that can't be priced in front of witnesses.

He came. He always comes. That is the thing about the disowned man: the file protects its own grammar, and he is the file's most fluent hand.

"They want to own the correction," the King said.

"Yes, Majesty." The advocate's hands were empty. They were always empty now. The empty hands had become his signature — the man who arrived with nothing because everything he touched had already been taken by somebody. "If the correction is property, I am its porter. The Order cannot fire me — the waterline oath. The sea cannot release me — the stipulation. But the Order can own what I carry, and ownership is a different leash."

Zee looked at him the way the King looks at instruments — the long, pricing look. "What does the correction say in the water's grammar?"

The advocate went very still. "Your Majesty. I have never been asked."

"Ask it now."

He read it. In the small chamber, in the tide-cadence, the correction — the clerk's substitution, the fileable name, the forgery the whole war ran on — spoken in the sea's own grammar. And the arithmetic of it, spoken aloud, was the coldest thing I had heard all winter, colder than the counterclaim, colder than the perjury, colder than the margin.

The correction, in the sea's grammar, was not a name. It was a debt receipt. The registry's corrected carrier-name — the fileable one, the one with a bearer and standing — named the *buyer* as the carrier. The grammar the intake clerk used to make the name fileable had, in the water's reading, inverted the custody: the name the sea carried for what it took became, in the file's grammar, the name of whoever carried the *record* of what it took.

The Order bought the receipt at salvage. The receipt carried the correction. The correction, in the sea's grammar, named the carrier.

The Order named itself the debtor.

The advocate finished reading and stood there with the chamber's walls holding the silence, and I watched the King's face, and the King smiled. It was not a warm thing.

"File it," Zee said.

The magistrate convened at the waterline at the fourth low tide. The advocate — guardian-designate of the carrier-name, sworn to the waterline, retained by the sea, fired by the Order, and now legally required by the registry to carry both versions of the name — stood in the tide and read the correction-of-correction in both grammars, in triplicate, with the registry's clerk present to certify what the water filed.

The Order's counsel objected. He objected in the dated grammar, in the tide's cadence, in the older liturgy — three objections, three grammars, all of them correct, all of them procedural, all of them saying the same thing: the correction is our property, the name is ours, the carrier is ours to name.

The magistrate looked at the water. Then at the Order's counsel. Then at the file.

"Resolved against the preparer," he said.

The oldest rule in the registry's grammar — the one the Order had spent thirty-four years teaching the file, the one it had filed against everyone else in every dispute it had ever won — came back the way all arithmetic comes back: on the hand that wrote it. The registry prepared the correction. The registry's forgery is resolved against the registry. The correction-of-correction stands: the intake's barnacled original is the name of record, and the corrected name — the fileable one, the face the Order carried on its books — is annotated *superseded by the preparer, resolved in favor of the drowned.*

The Order's buyback at salvage became, in one ruling, the evidence of its own loss. It had bought a forgery as genuine, carried it at face value, built its margin position on it, and the sea's counterclaim — the Order named debtor on the margin between discount and face — now read, in the corrected arithmetic, against the buyer, not the seller. The margin between a discount and a fiction is a debt on whoever paid the discount.

The Order paid the discount.

The bailiff came down to the waterline before the tide turned. I was there — the King's clerk is always where the dangerous filings are — and I saw the way the file will record it: the bailiff waded in with the seizure order, the Order's hand, seize the intake journal, seize the correction-of-correction, take the paper before the tide certified — and he laid his hand on the advocate's wrist, at the water's edge, with the guardian's seal still wet on the magistrate's ruling.

The King was standing in the corridor.

Zee moved the way Zee moves when procedure has finished. The bailiff's hand was on the advocate's wrist. Then the bailiff was in the water, facedown, in the docket the Order built, in the venue the magistrate constituted, in the tide that had just been handed a drawer of its own — and the water took him the way the water takes everything, without comment, without receipt, with the barnacled certainty of a creditor that has never once been wrong about who owes it.

The King did not touch him. That was the worst of it. The King stood in the corridor with his hands at his sides and the ash smell on the harbor wind and the heat under his skin that this city's prose will never name, and the tide did the arithmetic for him — because the venue's officer, the guardian, the only man standing in the water between the courts, had just watched his masters' bailiff lay hands on the registry's guardian-designate in the registry's own venue, and the file keeps that the way the file keeps everything.

The advocate finished the certification. The tide filed it. The clerk stamped it in triplicate, with wet boots, in the harbor wind.

The registry's arithmetic always balances.

It just found the man to balance on.
