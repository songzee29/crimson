# Chapter 157 — "Drovak Senn"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

Drovak Senn did not run, did not fortify, did not confess, and did not wait.

That was the thing about the last one. The other six had each chosen a posture — Varren's walls, Brannic's fist, Thoss's alley, Vane's money, Sera's mind, Vale's ledger — and every posture had been a kind of answer to the same question, asked in the same voice, on the same rainy nights. Senn chose nothing. Senn dismantled his life the way you dismantle a stage after the play: quietly, completely, and without any expectation that anyone would be watching.

Zee found the house in the Tremé with all its lights on and all its doors open.

Not unlocked. *Open.* Front door standing wide to the street, back door open to the courtyard, windows raised though the night was cold. The house had the particular emptiness of a place whose furniture has been given away rather than stolen — clean rooms, bare walls, the pale rectangles on the plaster where pictures had hung for years.

Senn sat at the kitchen table in the middle of it all, alone, with a bottle of whiskey he wasn't drinking and a stack of paper he was.

"Evening," Senn said, without looking up. "I left the doors open so you wouldn't have to use the walls. Vane said you came through his wall. I'd rather keep mine."

Zee stepped through the open doorway and stood in the kitchen and looked at the last of the eight. Senn was younger than the rest had been — forty, maybe, thin in the wrists, with the pale stare of a man who had spent his working life watching other people live theirs.

"The spotter," Zee said.

Senn nodded. "The eyes. I watched you for six weeks before the harbor. Where you walked. When you went home. Which door." He finally looked up. "I told them when to move. That's the whole of my part. I never held a knife. I held a *schedule*."

Zee didn't answer. He was looking at the paper. Neat handwriting, page after page, in the careful block letters of a man who had been a clerk before he was a conspirator.

"What's that?" Zee asked.

"The truth." Senn pushed the stack across the table. "Every night of the operation. Every name. Every payment I saw pass through my hands, and I saw more than the eight knew I saw. I wrote it for their families — Varren's widow, Brannic's mother, the rest of them. They deserve to know why their men died. They won't get it from the city." A pause. "I wrote it for yours, too. The parts about the watching. There's a page near the back you'll want."

Zee picked up the stack. He didn't read it. "Why give it to me?"

"Because you're the only one left who'll carry it anywhere." Senn's smile was thin and used up. "And because I spent six weeks watching you walk home to your family, Zee Darkheart, and I want somebody in this story to have seen it. Somebody who'll remember I saw it."

The hunger moved under Zee's ribs, restless, *disappointed*. It had fed on fire and spectacle for six weeks — Brannic's fist, Thoss's blood, Vane's heart, Sera's mind, Vale's burning whole — and it did not know what to do with a thin man at a kitchen table with the doors open. The wolf shifted, uncertain of the scent. The dragon heat barely stirred. Even the embers at the edges of everything seemed to breathe slower, bored.

This one wouldn't feed anything. This one would only end.

"Do you know who the bidder was?" Zee asked. He had to ask. He owed the asking to the six names already crossed out.

Senn shook his head. "I saw a man with an envelope, once. In a hotel lobby, three cities from here. Vale took it from him and didn't open it in front of me." He met Zee's eyes. "I can tell you this much: the man never watched the harbor. He watched the *watching*. He was checking that Vale was watching you right. Whoever paid for the demonstration didn't buy a view of your death, king." A thin pause. "He bought a view of *mine*. Of all of ours. We were the demonstration. Not you."

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"I have one request," Senn said. "Don't make it loud. My sister's boy reads the papers. He thinks I was an accountant." He pushed the whiskey bottle aside and folded his hands on the table, neat as his handwriting. "That's all. That's the whole negotiation."

Zee stood in the open doorway of the dismantled house and looked at the last man on the list, and the hunger *reached* — not for the throat, not for the heart, but for the spectacle it had been promised, the seventh fire, the seventh feast —

— and Zee denied it.

He crossed the kitchen in one step and put his hand on Drovak Senn's chest, and the witch-mana rose up out of the floorboards in crimson threads, quiet as dust, and went into the man's ribs the way light goes into a dark room. No claws. No fire. No wings. The threads found the heart and *held* it — one beat, two — and then gently, the way you'd close a book you'd finished reading, stopped it.

Senn's eyes stayed on his. There was no awe in them, the way there had been in Vane's, no calculation like Vale's. Just the pale stare, steady, and then the steadiness going out of it the way a lamp goes out when the oil is done.

He folded forward onto the table, onto his own pages, and was still.

The hunger *screamed* — denied, cheated, starving in the middle of a feast it couldn't taste — and the wolf snarled at the leash, and the dragon heat rolled once under the scales and subsided, and the embers at the edges breathed, and breathed, and breathed, patient as ever, and Zee stood in the quiet kitchen with his hand still on a dead man's chest and understood, with perfect cold clarity, what the last of the eight had done to him.

He'd made it small. He'd made it *nothing*. Six weeks of fire and blood and spectacle, and the end of it was a quiet kitchen and a man who'd asked only that it not be loud — and the hunger, denied its seventh feast, had nowhere left to go but inward.

*Seven,* Zee thought.

*Selk Varren. Brannic Dor. Vaeren Thoss. Kaldrek Vane. Sera Quill. Jorren Vale. Drovak Senn.*

*Seven.*

He took the stack of paper. He read the last page first — the page Senn had meant for him. It was shorter than the rest. Three lines, in the same careful block letters:

*The envelope man checked the watching, not the harbor. Whoever he works for wanted to see what the eight would do, not what you would. We were never the test. We were the questions on it.*

Zee folded the pages with great care and put them in his coat, next to his heart, next to Thoss's list and Vane's ghost and all the other paper the dead had left him.

He closed Senn's eyes. He straightened the collar the way you straighten a man's collar when you're sorry about the necessity of him. He blew out the kitchen lights one by one, and left the doors open the way he'd found them, and walked out into the Tremé with the pages in his coat and the hunger coiling under his ribs like a plucked string with no song left to play.

Behind him, the empty house kept its open doors, and the night came in, and nobody watched at all.

---

He didn't go home. Not yet.

He went to the levee instead, and stood with the river moving black and endless beside him, and read Senn's pages by the light of nothing — the witch-mana in his blood was enough, crimson letters floating in the dark — and he read about the eight the way you read an autopsy: Varren's engine, Brannic's fist, Thoss's knife, Vane's money, Sera's mind, Vale's ledger, Senn's eyes. Eight men, one harbor, one unkillable king. A demonstration.

*We were the demonstration. Not you.*

The hunger coiled. The wolf paced. The embers breathed.

And Zee stood on the levee with seven names crossed out and one name delivering ledgers to Vesper, and felt the window — not as an opening anymore, not even as a door off its hinges, but as a *room* he was already standing inside, with the walls gone and the night coming in from every side, and the only thing between the hunger and the house on the edge of the Quarter was the choice he made every hour, and the man who made it.

*Not yet,* he told the hunger. *Not yet.*

But the list was done. And the shape was gone. And underneath it, the hunger was still standing there, patient, with nothing left to aim it at —

— except him.
