# Chapter 182 — "The Toll, Requisitioned"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The requisition came the morning after the rota took the noble, sealed with the war-marshal's stamp, and it was addressed — in the marshal's own clerk-hand — to *the satchel-master of the nursery.*

Nine read it in the gate school's courtyard, at the bench where the certificates had gone to the broker's courier, and the shake stayed in his hands where anyone close could see it.

"The nursery," he said. Not to Tessa. To the paper.

Tessa had the war table's copy. She'd read it before he'd finished the first line. "They don't mean the Order's nursery," she said. "The Order's nursery doesn't take Crown requisitions. It *sends* them."

Nine looked up. "Then they mean us."

They meant them.

The war-marshal was drilling his men for both wars — the one against the Order's ghosts and the one against their salaries — and the drill field requisitioned orderlies. Field kitchens. The requisition named a number: twelve children. Age eight to fourteen. Able-bodied. To be furnished by the nursery of the gate school at toll price, the Crown paying the toll the way the house paid it — the road's roster, the roster's rate — filed as *final placement, military.*

Final placement. The nursery's grammar for a child who would not be coming back.

Nine sat down on the bench with the requisition in his hands, and the courtyard went on being morning around him — the eight-year-old running the count for the new arrivals, Rhyven at the gate with his hand flat on the warm stone, thirty children breathing in the dust — and the paper said the Crown wanted twelve of them for the kitchens of a war, priced the way the road priced the ones nobody claimed.

"The toll," Nine said quietly, "is what the road pays for a child nobody owns. We pay it to get them *out* of the price." He looked at Tessa. "They've turned it around. Now the Crown pays the toll to us, and the child goes *in*."

"It's worse than that," Tessa said. She held up the second sheet, the marshal's schedule. "The rota. Yesterday's taking. The noble, the house robe, the gallery. The war-council's minute is attached — the council read the rota's filing and wrote, in its own hand, that the King's household is now a *scheduled* institution. Scheduled institutions can be requisitioned. The institution that takes titled men on schedule, the council says, can furnish orderlies on schedule. The hunger set the precedent. The kitchens are collecting it."

Nine stood up. The shake ran through his hands, and he didn't hide it — the shake was his now, unfiled, uncounted, the way the courtyard had taught him.

"Then I refuse."

"The marshal expects the refusal," Tessa said. "It's in the covering note. *Refusal to be filed as debt against the toll.*"

The words sat between them the way warrants sat between them now — paper that had stopped being paper and started being weather.

Nine read the covering note. It was exact. The Crown's requisition was not a purchase. It was a *call* on the toll's accounting: the house had been paying the toll for the road's children for a season — the intake, the placements, the gate school's keeping — and every payment had gone into the registry's arithmetic. The toll was a Crown mechanism. The roster was the Crown's roster. The house's payments had bought the children out of the price, but the arithmetic was the Crown's, and the Crown was now calling the arithmetic due: twelve children, at toll price, furnished, or the refusal entered as a *debt against the toll* — the house owing the Crown children it would not sell, carried forward, interest compounding in the registry's own hand.

"They're not ours to sell," Nine said.

"That's the refusal," Tessa said. "And the refusal is the debt. That's the whole of the requisition, Nine. There is no answer that isn't filed."

---

He wrote it in the gate book, because the house filed everything, even refusals, even the moment the master of the count refused the count on the record. Tessa stood beside him with the ledger, the way she'd stood beside him for the contempt, and the pen moved the way it always moved — steady, exact, without flinching.

The refusal was not a speech. Nine had learned, in the courtyard, that speeches were the nursery's genre. He wrote three lines in his own hand, the hand the tutor had taught Zayrlen to read slowly, the hand the commission had tried to put on a master line beneath eleven wrong names:

*The nursery of the gate school holds no children for sale. The toll paid for a child is the price of a leaving, not the price of a body. Furnish nothing. File this.*

He signed it with the number the nursery had given him, because the nursery's name was the only signature the requisition would recognize — and then, underneath, he wrote the other name, the one he didn't have yet, the one the eight-year-old had promised to hold for him until he did:

*Nine. The leaving kind.*

Tessa sealed it for the marshal's courier with the house's own wax. The refusal went out the way the certificates had gone out — quietly, correctly, filed where the registry could read it.

The registry read it. The registry filed it.

*Refusal entered. Debt against the toll: twelve children, at toll price, carried forward.*

---

The eight-year-old found him at the bench afterward, the way she'd found him every evening since the morning she'd asked him to walk away.

"Was it a bad paper?" she asked.

"It was a paper," Nine said. "That's the badness of it."

"Are we the toll's?" She said it the way she'd said *they get thrown away* — matter-of-fact, road-truth, the grammar of a child who had learned the world's arithmetic before she learned its letters.

Nine looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "The toll is a price, not an owner. The Crown wants to make it an owner. I told them no."

"And the no is a debt."

"And the no is a debt." He didn't soften it. The house didn't soften true things anymore. "Which means the Crown owns the arithmetic of the no. Which means —" He stopped. He didn't finish, because the finishing of it was the hearing, six days out, and the advocate filing the debt alongside the contempt and the ash, and the Order arguing, in open session, that the household that refused to furnish its own nursery to the Crown was a household withholding Crown assets — children, priced, filed — and the dread of it was too big for the bench and too exact for the courtyard.

The girl sat down beside him on the bench, untaught, knees up, elbows on knees, the way the children sat now.

"Then we'll be the kind of debt that doesn't get collected," she said.

Nine almost laughed. It came out as breath instead. "Debts get collected, little one. That's what debts are."

"Not this one." She folded her small hands right — the hands he'd steadied, the wrists with the marrow-fractures healing crooked under Rhyven's wrap. "They can't collect children who know how to leave. You taught us the leaving. Teach it to the paper too."

---

Zee came down at dusk. The children were gone to supper. Nine sat alone on the bench with the registry's filing — the debt, in the registry's hand, twelve children carried forward — and the shake in his hands.

He stood when the King entered. He didn't let the posture go this time. He stood straight, the way a satchel-master stood when the satchel was empty and the paper was against him.

"My King," he said. "The Crown requisitioned twelve children for the field kitchens. At toll price. Filed as final placement, military."

"I know," Zee said. The tribrid stillness came over him the way weather comes over water. "The marshal brought it to the war table before it came here. I told him to send it."

Nine stared. "You —"

"I told him to send it to the satchel-master, not the war table," Zee said. "Because the war table would have furnished them. The war table furnishes things. That's what war tables are." He sat down on the bench beside the boy — untaught, knees apart, the way kings didn't sit. "The nursery that taught the leaving had to be the one to refuse. Not me. Me refusing is a King refusing his own council, and the registry files that as policy. You refusing is a master refusing a sale of his own children — and the registry has to file *that* as a debt, which means the registry has to admit, in its own hand, that the children were ever anybody's to refuse."

Nine looked at him. "The debt is twelve children. Carried forward. Interest compounding."

"I know."

"They'll collect it at the hearing."

"I know." Zee was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "The institution the house built to save children from the market just got classified as the market's supplier. And the refusal — your refusal, in your own hand, with the name you don't have yet under it — is the first invoice the house can't pay." He looked at the boy. "So we don't pay it. We let it sit in the registry's arithmetic, filed, compounding, and we let the advocate argue it in open session — and when he does, the whole court hears the Crown's own clerk read aloud that the King's household owes the Crown twelve children it refused to sell."

"The shame of it," Nine whispered.

"The *record* of it." Zee stood. The heat came up under the scales at the edges of everything, banked, unnamed — the fire that had no name in any registry, the wings that never showed, the burning that stayed banked because the page wasn't ready for the naming and the naming wasn't his to give. "Let the advocate argue with the debt. Let the magistrate read it. The household's own honesty keeps donating itself to the file — and this is the one donation that names the price and refuses to pay it."

He walked up toward the house, toward supper, toward the room with the door that locked from the inside. Nine sat on the bench with the registry's filing and the shake in his hands, and the road kept knocking behind him in the dark — a boy, a girl, another boy — and every knock was a name the Crown wanted priced, and every name was a leaving the school hadn't finished teaching.

But the master of the count had refused the count. The satchel-master had refused the sale. The King had burned the nursery's paper and signed the noble's warrant. And somewhere in the registry's file, a clerk was writing down the three newest pages: *contempt*, and *ash*, and now *debt* — twelve children, at toll price, carried forward, uncollectable.

The hearing was six days out. The advocate would file them all.

Let the advocate argue with the debt. Walls outrank knocks.
