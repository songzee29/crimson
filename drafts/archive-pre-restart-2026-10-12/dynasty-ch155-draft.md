# Chapter 155 — "Drovak Senn"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

Drovak Senn was the map.

That was what Zee worked out a week after Vale, sitting in the library with a pot of coffee gone cold and Ner asleep on the chaise with his head on a stack of shipping manifests he'd insisted on reading himself — *if I'm going to know what my husband is killing, I'm going to know all of it* — and Zee laid out the eight the way you lay out a hand of cards, face up, one by one.

Selk Varren built it. Brannic Dor swung it. Vaeren Thoss held the knife. Kaldrek Vane paid for it. Sera Quill planned it. Jorren Vale sold the tickets.

And someone had drawn the map they'd all moved through — the harbor's back alleys, Zee's walks, the blind turns where eight men could stand in the dark and not be seen from the street.

Ner had circled one name in gold ink, hours before he'd fallen asleep. "The harbor-master's brother," he'd said. "The one who knew the water the way you know the Quarter."

Drovak Senn.

*Seven,* Zee thought, and the hunger — which had been coiling inward since Vale, sharpening itself on the absence of names — rose up and *looked* at him.

---

Senn didn't run, didn't fight, didn't broker, didn't beg.

He burned.

Zee found him in the stilt house out past the Rigolets where the marsh went silver at dusk, a tin washtub in the yard full of ash and half-curled paper, and Senn standing over it with a kerosene can and eyes like a man who'd been burning his life for three days and still hadn't gotten to the part of it that mattered.

Maps. Every sheet was the harbor — Zee could see it in the curled edges, the contour of the basin, the cross-hatched alleys, the little red X's. Weeks of watching. A month of Zee's walks, reduced to ink and compass rose.

"You're fast," Senn said, without looking up. "I figured Vale would buy me ten days."

"Vale bought you seven."

Senn nodded like that was fair and poured kerosene over the last of the maps and struck a match. The fire went up orange and ordinary — real fire, the kind that didn't bow to anything — and Senn watched his work go with the particular tenderness of a craftsman killing his craft.

"I want to tell you something before you do it," Senn said. "Not to buy my life. There is no buying. I just want it said."

"Say it."

"My daughters are twelve and nine." Senn's voice didn't break. That was the worst part — it was *even*, the way a man talks when he's already rehearsed this to himself for a month. "They don't know what I drew. They think their father runs a bait shop and is bad at it. When you kill me — and you will, I drew your walks, I put the knife-hand at the blind turn, I've had a month to be afraid of this exact evening — I want you to let it look like the marsh took me. A boat. A bad night. Something their mother can bury without a story."

Zee stood at the edge of the yard with the marsh wind moving his coat and the horns catching the last of the light, and he felt the hunger stretch toward the man's throat like a dog straining at a fence, and underneath it the wolf, and underneath the wolf the heat moving through his scales, and at the edges of all of it the embers breathing, brighter every day now, like something asleep that was almost awake.

"No," Zee said.

Senn's jaw tightened. "Then they're orphans of a legend. Is that what you want on their doorstep?"

"I want what's true on their doorstep." Zee stepped into the yard. The ash from the washtub lifted around his boots. "Men like us don't get natural deaths, Drovak. We get honest ones. Your daughters will grow up knowing exactly what their father drew and exactly what it cost him, and they will be *safer* for it, because they will never once in their lives mistake a map for a neutral thing."

"That's cruelty."

"That's the only mercy I have left to give." Zee was close now. He could smell the kerosene on the man's hands. "You drew the route they used to jump a king. You didn't hold a knife, you held a *pen* — and the pen is how the eight of you stood in my harbor without me smelling you. So here's the truth for the doorstep: their father was good at what he did, and what he did was wrong, and the King of the Quarter came to his yard at dusk and did not lie about it."

Senn looked at him for a long moment, and then — the strangest thing — he *laughed*. Short. Surprised. "You know, I drew you for a month. Your walks, your turns, the nights you doubled back. And I never once drew that."

"Drew what?"

"That you'd let me talk." Senn set down the kerosene can. "The other six. Did any of them get to talk?"

"Vane talked. Vale talked. The rest chose their rooms."

"Then I choose mine." Senn straightened, marsh-man to the end, and lifted his chin. "Not the fire. I've watched your work, King. The fire's for the ones who sold the viewing. I'm a pen, not a broker."

Zee looked at him — at the steady hands, at the ash on the sleeves, at the daughters' ages written all over a face that had already rehearsed this — and the hunger screamed *throat*, and the wolf lunged, and the fire at the edges of him rose up like a held breath —

— and Zee put his hand on the back of Senn's neck and broke it clean.

One sound. Quick as a snapped branch. The body folded into the ash-yard, and the maps' smoke went up past the stilts, and the marsh went on being silver, indifferent as weather.

*Seven.*

The hunger didn't sing. That was the new thing, and Zee stood there with his hand still outstretched over the dead man and *noticed* it the way you notice a missing tooth — the hunger didn't sing, it *sharpened*, coiling in on itself, because there were no names left. Pell was gone, off somewhere delivering ledgers that arrived sealed and unread. Senn was ash and paper.

Seven down. One deferred. And the hunger, with nothing left to eat, was beginning to look at the city.

He walked back through the marsh with Senn's kerosene still on his hands, and he didn't wash them. He wanted the truth on his own doorstep, too.
