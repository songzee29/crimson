# Chapter 195 — "The Floor They Never Built"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

Vaeren Thoss had built against everything except the ground.

That was the intelligence, and it was the kind of intelligence that told you its own story if you read it the way an engineer reads a wall — which is how Thoss had wanted it read, because Thoss was a reader of walls. The war-marshal laid the drawings out on the war table the way men laid out autopsies: the curtain walls doubled since Dor's death, the moat tripled and chained, the gatehouse rebuilt around a murder corridor that could be flooded, starved, or dropped on anyone who walked it. The engineer's tower stood at the fortress's heart, and inside the tower, the engineer's room — stone four feet thick on every side, one door, no windows, a vault that was supposed to hold when everything else fell.

"He housed Varren," the war-marshal said. "When Zee burned Varren's men out of the low country, Thoss opened his gate and closed it behind them. Varren planned the jump from inside those walls."

Zee stood over the drawings with his hands flat on the table's edge, and his eyes were doing the arithmetic the war-marshal's men had stopped doing — the arithmetic under the arithmetic.

"Show me the bedrock," he said.

The war-marshal blinked. "The walls—"

"Are four walls," Zee said. "Walls I have met before. Show me what the walls are standing on."

The surveyor was brought in before the third bell. He had the old county maps, the ones drawn before Thoss's masons arrived, and he spread them over the engineer's drawings the way history spreads itself over ambition. The fortress stood on a limestone shelf — thick at the western rise, thinning as it fell toward the moat, and at the eastern curtain, under the highest tower, the shelf was not a shelf. It was a skin. Forty feet of stone and below it the karst, hollowed, wet, waiting.

"They built a wall against kings," Zee said, looking at the map the way other men looked at the wall. "Nobody built a floor."

---

The fortress of Vaeren Thoss sat on the shelf at dusk like a boast made of stone.

Zee walked up to it alone. That was the protocol now — the men of the war table had learned not to argue the walk. Rez kept the rear a half-league back with the riders, not because Zee needed keeping but because the king's guardian did what guardians did, and the distance was Rez's own negotiation with a duty that had outlived every war it was built for. The moat chains were drawn. The gatehouse waited with its flood and its starving and its drop.

Zee did not look at the gate.

He walked the fortress's perimeter the way an engineer walks a perimeter, and the Wolf in him counted the men's heartbeats on the wall — sixty, seventy, more behind the stone — and the Witch in him felt the bedrock under his boots, the shelf's thickness changing with every step, the map's truth in the soles of his feet. The Vampire in him was awake too, and it was quiet the way it was quiet when the work was certain, because the hunger had learned the difference between a thirst and an appointment.

At the eastern curtain, under the highest tower, he stopped. He put his palm flat against the wall.

The Dragon in him answered. It did not ask the stone for permission. It asked the stone what it was made of, and the stone answered — limestone, water-eaten, forty feet of it, and below it the hollow, and below the hollow the dark.

Thoss's men watched from the wall. They had arrows nocked and orders shouted, and the orders died in the orderers' mouths, because the king was standing at their wall with his hand on it, doing nothing, the way a mason stands at a wall when he is deciding where the crack goes.

"Lord Thoss!" the captain called down. "This ground is—"

"This ground," Zee said, without looking up, "is about to be something else."

He knelt. He set both palms on the earth at the curtain's foot, and the Witch in him reached down through the soil the way roots reach down, and found the shelf, and held it — the whole forty feet of it, the way you hold a man's wrist before you break it.

Then the fire came up.

Not through his hands. *Down* through them. The burning that lived under everything he was — the fire that had no name he would ever speak, the wings he never unfurled where anyone could see — ran out of his palms and into the stone the way a river runs downhill, and it did not burn the wall. It burned the floor the wall was standing on.

The captain on the curtain stopped shouting. The men on the wall stopped drawing bows. They stood and watched the ground at the king's knees begin to glow the color of forge-iron, and they watched the curtain wall — four feet of masoned stone, doubled since Dor's death — lean.

It leaned a degree. Then a second.

Inside the fortress, in the engineer's tower, Vaeren Thoss was looking at his wall charts when the floor of his tower moved under his boots.

He looked up. His maps were sliding on their table.

And Vaeren Thoss, the engineer who had built against everything, understood for the first time what his enemy had understood from forty feet of air.

Nobody had built a floor.
