# Chapter 150 — "The Last Copy"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

"Kill me first," Sera Quill said. "Then read the copies. That's the price of the answer."

Zee stood ten paces from her in the empty paper mill and considered the woman who had planned his almost-death. Rain drummed on the roof forty feet above them. The work-light buzzed. Somewhere outside, the city breathed.

She hadn't raised the gun. She wouldn't. That was the thing about the smart ones — they'd already done the math, and the math said the gun was a prop, and props were for people who still believed in the scene.

"You're not going to bargain for your life," Zee said. It wasn't a question.

"No." She lowered the gun to her side, finger off the trigger, the gesture of someone laying down a tool. "I planned the harbor, Zee. I drew the sightlines and timed the fist and put the knife where your spine would be. Your code doesn't have a chair for me at the table. I know that." The white wing in her hair caught the light. "I'm bargaining for the *copies*. They go to Vale in the morning if I don't stop them. You have until then to find three locations. I can give you all three right now — or you can spend the night hunting paper while the clock runs."

"And the price of the answer." His eyes held hers, crimson-gold, banked coals. "The order. What Victor paid for."

"What *someone* paid for." The correction was gentle, precise, and it landed like a second blade. "Vane told you *funded*. Vane was a money man — money men see money everywhere. The list said *testing, not killing*. Those are different sentences, Zee. I know which one I wrote down. The copies know which one is true."

The hunger coiled under his ribs, interested now, listening. *Testing, not killing* — Thoss's list. *Funded as a test* — Vane's claim, dying, desperate, maybe true. And Sera standing between the two sentences like a woman holding a door.

"Where are they?" Zee asked.

She told him. Three locations, no hesitation, no code words — a storage unit, a safety-deposit box, a dead drop in a cemetery she'd chosen for the joke of it. Names, numbers, the check-in protocol. She gave it all the way a planner gives everything: completely, because partial was just another kind of lie.

"Why?" Zee asked.

"Because you're going to kill me anyway, and I'd rather be the woman who finished the job than the woman who begged." She set the gun on the concrete and stepped away from it. "And because —" She stopped. For the first time the steadiness cracked, just a hair. "Because Vale was going to let the switches go live. Not to hurt you. To *use* them. Proof of everything, in the right hands, at the right time — that's not insurance, Zee. That's inventory. I built the copies to protect myself. He was going to sell them."

Zee was quiet. The rain kept falling on the roof. The shadows behind him moved out of sync with the light.

"You were the only one who understood," he said finally. "The white hair. You saw it that night."

"I saw that we'd never been the hunters." Her smile was thin and real. "It seemed worth marking."

He crossed the ten paces. She didn't step back. That was the last thing he respected about her — that she stood, the way Thoss had stood, the way brave people stand when the math is done.

"Close your eyes," he told her, the way he'd told the markswoman on Royal Street.

This time, she did.

It was quick — the price of bravery in his court. His hand went through her sternum and closed around her heart and it was over before the work-light buzzed twice. But he didn't stop there, because the hunger had been *listening*, because it had been awake since 3:12 and it was done being patient — and when her heart stopped, Zee bent to her throat and *fed*, and the fire came with it.

It rose off his shoulders in wings of shadow and burning light, vast in the empty mill, and the rain coming through the broken skylight hissed to steam where it struck them, and the concrete under his knees scorched black in the shape of something with wings. Sera Quill's blood hit his tongue like a struck bell — hot, iron, *clever*, even her blood tasted clever — and the hunger roared up through him exultant, and the dragon blood answered with heat that rolled off his scales in visible waves, and at the edges of it all the embers breathed, patient, waiting.

He had no name for it. He didn't need one.

When it was done he laid her down on the concrete gently, and closed her eyes, and straightened her collar the way you straighten a collar when you're sorry about the necessity. Small mercies. He was full of small mercies. It was the large ones that never came.

Then he went and found the three locations before morning.

---

The storage unit was first. The safety-deposit box was second — the night manager at the bank took one look at Zee's face and opened the vault without being asked, and then went home early and never spoke of it again. The cemetery was last, under a stone for someone named *Quill* — her mother, maybe; Zee didn't ask the stone.

By dawn the copies were his. Every one of them. The money, the orders, the bids. Four shell chapels and a funeral home and every dollar Jorren Vale had ever moved, written down in Sera's precise hand.

And the order — the true one, the one she'd written down.

Zee read it in the gray light before sunrise, standing in the rain outside the cemetery with death still on his coat, and his face did not change. Whatever the copies said about *funded* versus *authorized*, about testing versus killing — he folded the papers with great care and put them in his coat, next to his heart, next to Thoss's list.

*Five,* he thought. *Selk Varren. Brannic Dor. Vaeren Thoss. Kaldrek Vane. Sera Quill.*

*Five.*

He walked home through the rain, and the hunger walked with him, sated and *singing* — worse sated than starving, every feeding teaching it the shape of being fed — and the embers at its edges breathed a little brighter now, and Zee understood with perfect cold clarity that the window wasn't approaching anymore.

It was *open*.

Somewhere, his father was watching. *Testing, not killing.* Thirty-four years of watching, and now the test had a window, and the window had a shape, and the shape was Zee himself, walking home in the rain with five names counted and the fire on his shoulders and the hunger singing under his ribs.

He wiped the blood from his face and went inside, and Ner was waiting — not in the lamplight this time but in the dark of the hallway, arms folded, gold mana turned down low — and Ner looked at him, and didn't flinch, and that was the thing that nearly broke him, every time, that Ner never flinched.

"Five," Zee said quietly.

"I know." Ner took his hands — blood and all — and held them. His thumb moved over Zee's knuckles, warm, steady. "The copies?"

"Burned." A beat. "Copied first. Then burned."

Ner's mouth curved, just barely. "The planner would approve."

"She did." Zee closed his eyes. "She asked me to kill her first. So she could be the woman who finished the job."

They stood there in the dark hallway while the rain fell outside and the hunger sang under Zee's ribs and the embers breathed at its edges, and Ner held his bloodied hands, and the king darkness chose became, for a little while, just a man come home from the rain.
