# Chapter 183 — "The Earlier Slot"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The second petition arrived at the third bell, sealed with a different house's wax, delivered not by a courier but by the house's steward herself — a woman old enough to remember when the seal had meant something else, before it had started meaning petitions about the King's appetite.

Tessa read it at the war table with the warrant book open beside her, the way the house read everything now — slowly, completely, the way you read a thing that is about to ask something of you.

"It's not a claimant," she said. "It's not a creditor."

"Another penitent," Zee said. He didn't look up from the rota's weekly lines. His own signature sat on each one, in his own hand, the way the warrants had gone for a season.

"The same words," Tessa said. "Verrain's words. His hand was practiced. Hers is copied."

She laid it down. The petitioner wrote in the grammar of a woman who had learned the sentence from someone else's paper and filled it in the way you fill a form that has already decided what you mean:

*I am Odessa Marlowe, widow of the steward-line of House Marlowe. I ask to die by the King's hand, on the King's rota, witnessed, as an honor. Voluntary. Penitent. In my own name.*

Rez read it over Tessa's shoulder. His blue-white calm did its work on the room, lowering the temperature by being in it. "Steward-line," he said. "Not the blood. The service."

"Widow of it," Tessa said. "She married the house's steward and outlived him, and the house kept her in the seating out of — the note says *honor.* The family's honor." She paused. "Sixty-one. Sound of body, the physician writes. No sickness. No certificate."

Zee looked up at that. "No certificate."

"None. She isn't dying. She's —" Tessa turned the second page. The house's demand was itemized the way houses itemized everything now, in a steward's hand, beneath the petitioner's own: a date, a guest list, and the request, underlined twice, that the taking be set *ahead of the schedule* — before the hearing, before the advocate could speak of the house in open session.

"Before the hearing," Zee said flatly.

"The house fears the hearing's grammar," the war-marshal said. He had entered with the council's reading the way the council brought all its readings now — late, and already argued. "The advocate will stand in open session and argue that the King's Tithe is an unregulated appetite wearing a warrant. House Marlowe does not wish to be named, at that session, as kin of the petition the King has not yet answered." He laid his hand flat on the table. "So the house asks for speed. A taking before the speaking. An honor filed before the shame."

"And if I refuse the speed?" Zee asked.

The marshal's hand stayed flat. "Then the refusal is not a refusal of the petition, Majesty. The petition stands. The refusal is of the *expedition* — the earlier slot. And the house will file that refusal as a *claim*."

The room went quiet the way the war table's rooms went quiet now — paper listening to paper.

"A claim," Tessa said.

"A claim," the marshal agreed. "Not a policy. A grievance, with a standing. House Marlowe will argue, at the hearing, that the King's hunger keeps a calendar but the King cannot be hurried — that the appetite is still *his*, still chosen, still a hunger that picks its company by rank and by convenience. The registry will read it the way the registry reads everything: filed, numbered, argued."

Zee was very still. "Grant the earlier date, and the rota keeps appointments."

"Yes."

"Refuse it, and the hearing gets an exhibit proving the hunger has a favorite chair."

"Yes." The marshal did not soften it. "Verrain was the petition. Marlowe is the schedule. The institution Zee built as weekly penance has acquired a second thing it never asked for —" he gestured at the antechamber door, where the Verrain robe now hung, cleaned and folded, left by the Verrain steward in the exact care of a man leaving something *in case* — "a vestry, and a booking office, and a docket of its own."

Rez's blue-white calm had gone somewhere colder. "The hunger is learning to wear a title," he said, "and now it is learning to keep one."

---

That evening Tessa brought the warrant book to the rota chamber's antechamber, because the taking was three days out and the robe needed to be measured against the light, the way vestments got measured.

The Verrain robe hung on its peg. The Marlowe robe — a heavier garment, a widow's cut, the heirloom of a steward-line that had married into the seating — lay folded on the bench beside the steward's note, itemizing nothing, because the house had learned from Verrain's petition what a robe was for now: the garment a titled man or woman wore to be taken correctly.

Tessa stood between the two robes for a long moment.

"She isn't dying," she said to Zee, who had come down with her. "Verrain was dying. The sickness had done half the work and wanted the King's hand for the credit. Odessa Marlowe is sound of body. She wrote *penitent* in a hand that copied the word from someone else's paper, and the form doesn't ask what she's penitent for."

"The registry doesn't ask," Zee said. "That's the whole of the institution."

"Then what is she buying?"

Zee looked at the two robes — the folded one and the hung one — the way he looked at the warrants now: the way a man looks at paper that has outrun him.

"An earlier slot than the grief behind her," he said. "The house fears being named at the hearing as kin of an unanswered petition. The honor is the house's. The throat is hers."

"The throat is hers," Tessa repeated. She opened the warrant book. The weekly lines waited. Beneath Verrain's closed line — the name, the house, the hour, *voluntary, witnessed, penitent*, the hour filed — she ruled a new line, and wrote the name in the registry's hand.

*Odessa Marlowe. House Marlowe. The rota's hour, three days hence. Voluntary, witnessed, penitent.*

She read it back once, the way the house read everything.

"The nursery," she said quietly, not looking up. "The debt. The registry's twelve children, carried forward. If the council confuses the rota with the debt —"

"It won't."

"How do you know?"

Zee's stillness did the work the blue-white calm couldn't. "The rota takes the willing dead," he said. "The nursery holds the living. If the registry puts one child's name on the rota, I burn the page and the hand that wrote it."

Tessa's pen stopped. "That's not procedure, Majesty."

"No." The tribrid stillness banked underneath his voice, the way it had banked since the first loss — darker now, the darker that had been in him since the retaliation season, the ruthless that had learned to sit beside the protective without asking either for permission. "It's the boundary. The institution can have its vestry, its booking office, its docket. It cannot have the children."

He left the antechamber. The two robes hung and folded in the lamplight, and the warrant book sat open on the bench with the two lines — one closed, one open — and the hunger, patient, was learning to keep a calendar the way it had learned to wear a title: one titled throat at a time.
