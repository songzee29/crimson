# Chapter 213 — The Tide's Auditor

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

The treasury's appointment arrived at the harbor on a gray morning, and it arrived as arithmetic.

The auditor came alone, which was how you could tell he was serious. No guards. No clerk of his own. One man in a treasury coat, with a registry warrant folded in his breast pocket and a brass caliper on his belt, and the whole of the Crown's collection law riding behind his eyes. He had been sent to do what the lien demanded and what no one else in the city could do: assess the asset.

He could not audit a defendant. There was no defendant. There was a harbor, a dredge record, three days of slack water itemized as standing inventory, a shore-house letter that predated the Crown's charter, and a king who owned the sea the way a man owns a wound — by having paid for it in full and never being rid of it.

So the auditor audited the harbor.

He began with catch, because catch was the one thing the treasury already knew how to count. The dredge crews had been sworn at dawn, same as before, and the buckets came up out of the drowned channel and broke the surface, and the auditor stood at the quay with his caliper and his ledger and priced them the way the law demands everything be priced: by the bucket, by the tide, by the hour of the tide's slack.

Zee watched him work. The King had come down to the quay in a plain coat, no guard, no procession — the owner, attending his own assessment, as the law requires. He said nothing. He stood with his arms folded and let the numbers accumulate, the way a man lets a tide come in, because Ink's arithmetic had already told him what the auditor would find, and Ink was never wrong about arithmetic.

Catch, in crowns. The auditor's hand was steady. Buckets of silt, seal-fragments, shell. The Crown's share, itemized, totaled, carried forward. A number. A real number. The treasury could spend a number.

Spoil, in crowns. The sealed buckets the Crown had chartered — every one the shore had already spoken for, per the dredge-master's letter, but the law still priced them. The auditor priced them. The number went down in the ledger beside the catch, and the column stood up the way columns do when they know what they are: money, owed, counted.

Then the foam.

The auditor stood at the water's edge with his caliper and looked at the thin lace of gold foam still curling at the stones — Nhalaris's mark, the shore's signature, the thing the quay had seen and understood — and the caliper hung in his hand, useless, because you cannot caliper a signature. He wrote it down anyway, in the registry's hand, because the treasury's arithmetic has a line for everything, and the line he wrote was the one the whole war had been building toward.

*Foam: assessed as tide-mark. Value: unpriceable.*

He moved on to the slack water.

Three days of it. Itemized as standing inventory. The harbor held it the way a wound holds pressure — slack, still, docketed. The auditor measured it by the hour and found the hours would not stay. He measured it by the bucket and found the buckets would not hold still water the way the treasury needed buckets to hold it. Slack water, in inventory, is an asset the way a held breath is an asset: it exists, it is real, and it is gone the moment you claim to have taken it.

The auditor stood very still for a long time, with his ledger open, and then he did the thing no auditor in the history of the Crown's treasury had ever done. He converted the whole column.

Catch: in crowns. Spoil: in crowns. Foam: unpriceable. Slack water: a season.

And beneath it, in the registry's hand, in the hand the file would read for a hundred years:

*Total assessed revenue: in tides.*

The report landed in the registry's hand that afternoon. The treasury's clerks read it twice, the way men read things that are trying to kill them. The arithmetic was perfect. The arithmetic was complete. The arithmetic could not be spent.

You cannot garnish a season. You cannot bank a tide. You cannot pay a marshal in slack water and you cannot pay a debt in foam, and the auditor had done his job exactly — he had put a number on the ocean, and the number was a season, and the season was the asset, and the arrears were payable in the only currency the asset produced.

Zee read the report in chambers, with the auditor standing before him and the magistrate's seal still wet on the filing.

"Is it exact?" Zee asked.

The auditor — alone, serious, with clean hands and a haunted face — nodded. "As exact as the harbor allows."

"Then sign it as the assessor."

The auditor signed.

Zee took the pen. The King who had bought the sea to save it, the man the Crown was billing for the sea's arrears, signed the report as owner. He filed it himself, in triplicate, in the registry's hand, with the auditor watching and the magistrate's clerk stamping each copy.

The Crown's own tax lien now had a filed assessment. Signed by the assessor. Signed by the debtor. Stating, on the record, in the file's own grammar: the asset's revenue is in tides. The arrears are payable in tides. The treasury's arithmetic cannot spend what the asset produces.

The auditor left with his clean hands. He did not look back at the harbor. Men who have priced the unpriceable do not look back — they already know what they are walking away from, and it is the look of the thing that follows them home.

Zee walked up the quay alone. Behind him, the file closed on the report the way the sea closes on a stone: completely, and without a trace of the hand that signed it.

The file finally had a number on the ocean.

The number was a season.

And the King had signed it.
