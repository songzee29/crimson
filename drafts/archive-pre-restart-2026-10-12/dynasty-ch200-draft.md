# Chapter 200 — "The Owner's Second Counsel"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The termination arrived in chambers in a hand the registry could not price.

It came on nursery paper — the small, square, ruled sheets the gate school's children used — in a clerk's careful hand that wrote the Crown's letters the way the Crown wrote them, because someone had taught the hand. The motion was one page. The one page ended the Order's landlord-agency for the ruin's freehold, in full:

*Breach of duty. The agent litigated against its own tenant, billed the tenant's eviction, kept the tenant's coin. The agency is terminated, by the owner, by counsel, in right.*

The advocate read it standing, because the paper didn't want a chair — and the advocate read it three times, because the third line named the counsel of record, and the name was the whole of what was wrong with it.

*Counsel to the owner: the tutor.*

The man who had resigned Victor's court. The man the Order had once placed in the heir's schoolroom to teach the boy to read people. The man the file had watched walk out of a king's service with his hands clean and his grammar intact.

The Order's own former placement. Lawyering against it.

---

The tutor's first filing for the owner was not the eviction.

The eviction had been the Order's game — filed by the Order, for the Order, wearing the landlord-agency like a coat. The tutor filed the opposite of a coat. He filed the stone.

The ruin's freehold paid rent. That was the fact the file had been walking around for a season without looking at: the stone received the coin monthly, minted ahead of the calendar, unclaimed, uncounted, sitting in the registry's unclaimed drawer like a question nobody had filed. A landlord nobody could name, paying rent to nobody, in advance of time itself.

The tutor's motion was four lines, in the registry's own grammar, and the four lines did what no filing in the whole paper war had done:

*The owner moves that the unclaimed rent be received into escrow, pending naming of the receiver. The rent is the owner's. The file will hold it. The file will name the hand that takes it.*

Tessa read it standing. The war-marshal's hand went flat.

"Escrow," she said.

"Escrow," the tutor said from the gallery — he had come himself, the way men came when the filing was the whole of what they carried — "requires a receiver. A receiver requires a name."

The room went quiet the way chambers went quiet — paper listening to paper.

The Order's advocate stood. And the Order's advocate did the coldest thing the Order had done in the whole paper war — he moved to *quash* the escrow. He moved to keep the owner's money unclaimed. He argued, on the record, in chambers, in the Order's black, that the landlord must remain nameless.

"For the tenant's safety," he said. "For the household's safety. The naming of the owner would expose the freehold to claims this court has not yet heard. The Order opposes the escrow, and asks the court to continue the rent in the unclaimed drawer, where the law has kept it, correctly, all season."

Tessa watched him argue for the mask to stay on. She watched the advocate — the Order's man, who had billed the tenant's eviction in the landlord's name — tell the magistrate that the landlord he served could not be named, for the tenant's own good.

The mask was where the Order kept its hand. The advocate was asking the court to hold the mask on, in the Order's own motion, on the record.

The tutor didn't object. He didn't need to. He stood in the gallery in his plain coat, the man who had taught a boy to read people, and he let the file do the reading.

The magistrate heard them both out. Then the magistrate did the arithmetic the registry's arithmetic always did.

"Escrow granted," he said. "The rent is received into the court's keeping."

He set the paper down.

"The receiver," he said, "shall be entered in a sealed book. The book is the registry's. The name goes in, sealed, unfiled, unread. Neither party reads it. The court holds it."

The tutor bowed — the scholar's bow, the way men filed respect.

The advocate bowed — the advocate's bow, the way men filed knives — and sat down in the Order's black with his hands flat on his knees, and did not move, because there was nothing left to file.

---

Tessa filed the ruling the way the house filed everything now. The war table read it the way the house read everything — slowly, completely, the way you read a thing that is about to ask something of you.

The owner had counsel now. Not the Order's counsel — the tutor. The owner had terminated the agency that had been billing in its name, and the agency's own former placement was lawyering against it, and every trick the Order had taught the tutor in the enemy's handwriting, the tutor had learned in the heir's schoolroom, where the boy had taught him the rest.

The rent was in escrow. The receiver's name was in the sealed book. The file held a name neither side could read — and the file held everything it couldn't place, which meant the file now owned the landlord the way it owned everything else: correctly, completely, and sealed.

And the stone kept receiving the coin monthly, minted ahead of the calendar, the way it always had — only now the coin went into the court's keeping, and the court's keeping had a book, and the book had a name, and the name had a tutor watching it, and the Order's whole landlord game — the evictions filed in the landlord's name, the rent kept as the agent's own — sat in the file like a confession the registry hadn't read yet.

Every door the King closed became a filing someone else could price.

The Order had closed its own door this time — terminated by the owner it had been billing — and the price was a sealed book, a tutor's hand, and a landlord who had learned to use counsel the way kings used armies.

The first army the owner ever fielded was the enemy's.
