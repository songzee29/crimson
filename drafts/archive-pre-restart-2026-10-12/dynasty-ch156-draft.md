# Chapter 156 — "Zerlin"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The blackout began the way all the old ones had, which was the lie of it — it began with Zee being careful.

Three nights after Senn, he did the inventory of himself the way a man checks a wound: the hunger sharpened to a wire, the wolf pacing the edges of his ribs, the dragon heat coming off his scales in weather, the embers at the edges breathing in and out like something asleep that was almost awake. No names left. Pell offstage, delivering ledgers into a house Zee hadn't opened yet. The city outside, full of throats.

So he did what he'd done the night of Vale — he went to the empty house on the Quarter's edge, the dust-sheeted one, and he locked the doors from the inside, and the windows, and he sat on the floor in the dark and tried to outwait his own blood.

It didn't work. It had never worked.

What was different this time: he asked Rez to keep Ner away.

He'd found Rez in the stairwell at dusk — blue-white mana, calm as cut glass, the only man in the world who'd watched Zee's hungers for twenty-four years and never once flinched — and said, "Don't let him come."

Rez looked at him for a long moment. "He'll hate that."

"He'll live through it."

"Will you?" Rez asked quietly.

Zee didn't answer, and Rez — who was Zee's best friend and not his subject, which meant he could say the things subjects couldn't — nodded once and went downstairs to stand in front of the door like a wall.

The hunger *howled*.

It went through the locked house like weather. It went through Zee like weather. The wolf broke the leash first — he felt it go, felt the bones shift, the old wild grief of it — and then the hunger, and then the heat, and then the embers rose off his shoulders in wings of shadow and burning light, vast in the dark room, and the dust sheets *breathed* with it, and Zee's last clear thought before the dark took the edges of his vision was a stupid, ordinary one:

*I told Ner I'd try.*

---

He woke on a roof.

Dawn. The Quarter below him, wet and gold. Blood on his mouth — not his — and his hands, and a torn strip of somebody's shirt knotted around his forearm where he'd *tied* it, the way you tie a tourniquet, like some part of him had been doing triage even through the blackout.

He didn't know whose roof. He didn't know whose blood. The hunger was quiet — not sated, not singing, *spent*, the way a storm is spent, coiled down at the bottom of him with its eyes closed.

*No,* he thought, and sat up too fast, and the city tilted, and he made himself look.

The roof belonged to a gambling den off Dumaine — he knew the shape of the building the way you know a thing you've passed a thousand times without entering. Vane's money had run through it. He knew that because the ledgers Pell had been delivering — sealed, unread, stacked in the study like a debt — had names in them he'd never let himself read, and one of them, apparently, some blacked-out, starving, *unleashed* part of him had read anyway, or remembered, or simply *smelled*.

He went down the fire escape on legs that weren't quite his and found the den's back door hanging off its hinges, and inside —

Four men. Drained white. Arranged, almost, the way bodies aren't supposed to be arranged — one slumped over the faro table with his head on his arms like he'd fallen asleep, one against the wall with his hands open, empty, the way a man's hands go when he's surprised. No screaming in the plaster. No fire. The throat, and the dark, and the thing Zee became when no one was choosing.

The fifth man was alive. Tied to a chair with his own belt, shaking, eyes fixed on Zee's face the way a man looks at weather — not at a person, at *weather* — and he whispered, "You're the King," and then, "Please," and Zee understood with a cold, clean horror that the man had *watched*. Had watched the King come through the door in the dark and take the room the way a tide takes a beach, and had lived because the hunger had spent itself on four and left the fifth breathing.

Zee crossed the room. The man's pulse hammered in his throat, close enough to smell, and the hunger — spent, coiled, eyes closed — opened one eye.

"No," Zee said to it. Out loud. The man flinched. "Not him."

*He saw you,* the hunger said, in the voice it used when it was being reasonable, which was the most dangerous voice it had.

"He sees a king," Zee said, and untied the man's belt with hands that were still shaking, and the man fell out of the chair onto the floor and scrambled backward until his back hit the wall. "You saw a debt collected," Zee told him. "Vane's money ran through this room. The eight are dead. The debt is *done*. You walk out of here and you tell the rest of Vane's men exactly that: the ledger is closed, the King has collected, and anyone who opens a new one dies in the dark without a story." He crouched, so their eyes were level. "And if you tell anyone what you saw — the wings, the dark, *any* of it — I will come back, and next time I won't be the one who unties the chair."

The man nodded so hard Zee thought his neck would snap. Then he ran.

Zee stood in the den with four dead men and the dawn coming through the broken door and the truth of it settling into his bones like cold water: the blackout hadn't just taken him somewhere. It had taken him *somewhere specific*. To Vane's money. To the eight's machinery. The starving thing inside him hadn't gone hunting at random — it had gone to *finish the list*.

*Hunger or family,* his father had said through thirty-four years of watching. *Let's see what you choose.*

He'd chosen — and the choice had walked him through a gambling den in the dark and left four men drained white on the floor.

He went home the way you go home when you've done something you can't unsay. The front door. The stairs. The bedroom, where Ner was asleep with the dawn on his face and one hand still curled around the place where Zee's head would have been.

Zee stood in the doorway with another man's blood dried under his fingernails and looked at the gold of him, and the hunger — spent, coiled, one eye open — looked too.

And Zee did the thing the hunger never expected: he walked in, sat on the edge of the bed, and woke Ner up, and told him. All of it. The roof. The den. The four men. The fifth, untied. The choice.

Ner listened with his eyes wide and his hand over Zee's mouth before Zee could finish — not to stop him, to *hold* him — and then he said the thing that broke the morning open:

"Rez told me you asked him to keep me away."

"Yes."

"Don't ever do that again." Ner's voice was quiet and absolute. "You don't get to decide what I'm allowed to survive, Zee. That's mine. Not yours." He pulled Zee down, blood and all, and held him the way he'd held him at midnight after Vale — like a man holding a door shut against weather. "The next time the dark comes, you lock the house and you let me stand inside it with you. Or you lock me out and I break the door. Those are the choices. There is no third one."

Outside, on the front step where the morning post had come, lay a single black feather.

No card. No words. Nothing the registry could file, nothing a ledger could count.

Just a feather, on the doorstep, in the dawn.

Zee picked it up, and held it a long time, and said nothing at all.
