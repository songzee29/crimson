# Chapter 227 — The Guardian, Sued

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

The guardianship hearing opened Tuesday in the nine minutes.

Not in chambers. The magistrate had ruled the venue at the close of the signature hearing, and the ruling had the whole registry's clerks working through the night: a sealed signatory that cannot answer summons requires, under binding law, a guardian of the hand. Two claims were filed. The Crowns' custody appointee filed first — the registry's own instrument, filed, numbered, proper. The older instrument's liturgy filed second, claiming the sea itself as the carrier's guardian by older law: the sea carried the body, the sea keeps the drowned, and by the older grammar the carrier of the body is the guardian of the hand.

The registry agreed to hear both claims.

That was the motion the whole case had been waiting for without knowing it. Hearing both claims meant summoning the sea — and a party, unlike a deponent, can be cross-examined.

The advocate for the Order had filed for it. He had wanted the sea as a party since the second tide. He had gotten what he wanted the way men always get what they want in this file: by filing for the thing beside it.

And the venue — the magistrate's ruling on the venue was the coldest procedural arithmetic I had heard all winter. A party is examined in its own jurisdiction. The sea's jurisdiction is ocean time. The gap — the nine minutes, Crown property since the gap ruling, running on the sea's own reckoning, never ceded — is the only ground the court holds that sits on the water's hours. So the cross-examination of the ocean would sit in the gap, under Crown seal, on time the file owned but did not run.

The first hearing the file ever held on hours the file couldn't read.

The court assembled on the sand at the turn of the tide. The magistrate. The clerk, with his invented tide-footnote grammar and his marginal marks for the parts no seal can bind. The Order's advocate. The King's counsel. The custody appointee, with his numbered file. And the sea, summoned, arriving the way the sea always arrives — without arriving, already there.

I stood by the exits. There are no exits on a beach. I stood anyway. It is what I do.

The questions were filed in the clerk's tide-footnote grammar — *deponent, the sea* — the invented code the registry can never cite again. Whatever the ocean said here, the minutes would be the first court record of a cross-examination the file could never quote. The Order's counsel would file a land-word translation as the operative record, and the magistrate would adopt it, and the King would win the case and lose the paperwork — we had all read that arithmetic in the Minute, Unusable. We filed the questions anyway. Parchment law says the file *is* the case, and the file had run out of other rooms.

The advocate went first, because the advocate always goes first.

His questions were the Order's questions, and the Order's questions had one destination: the author. He wanted the alteration attributed to the sea. If the ocean altered the carrier-name transaction, the ocean was the author, and the author was liable, and the second tide was not a phenomenon — it was a confession.

"Did the sea," the advocate asked, in the clerk's grammar, "alter the carrier-name transaction it carried?"

The sea maintained its sworn silence.

It was the same silence the tide had sworn at the waterline — *declined to claim its carrier on the record*, sworn, stamped, in front of witnesses. The silence had a shape now. It was not the silence of a deponent who would not speak. It was the silence of a party that had decided which questions it would answer, and filed the decision the way the sea files everything: by being the water.

The advocate pressed. He filed three more questions, each one a narrower net, each one the same water. The silence held. I watched him understand, in the way I watch men understand things in courtrooms, that the cross-examination had a boundary he could not cross with questions — because the sea was not refusing to answer. It was refusing the *question's standing*. A party that declines to claim its carrier cannot be asked, as a party, to un-claim it. The grammar wouldn't hold the question. The clerk's marginal marks filled the page.

The King's counsel rose. He had one question. He had been carrying it since the drowned pages came up from the water, and I had priced it the day he filed it, because it was the only question in the whole case that didn't need the author.

"Did the carrier," he asked, "drown before or after the alteration was made?"

The sea answered.

The answer came in tide-cadence, and the clerk rendered it in the only land-words the invented grammar permitted, his hand shaking the way clerks' hands shake when the grammar gives them something the registry can never use:

The alteration was on the page when the page went down. The carrier signed an altered transaction and drowned on it.

The courtroom — the sand, the water, the nine minutes — went the way courtrooms go when the arithmetic lands. The advocate's net had caught the one thing his client couldn't use. The sea had not altered the transaction. The sea had *carried* it. The carrier doesn't write. The carrier carries. And the drowned hand — the sealed signatory the magistrate had bound to the file the day before — had signed paper that was already altered, and drowned on the signature, and the ocean had kept the page the way the ocean keeps everything: without reading it.

The guardian question collapsed in the same hour.

The magistrate laid the two claims on the clerk's table — on the sand, at the waterline, in the nine minutes — and did the arithmetic out loud, because the file required it.

The Crowns' custody appointee could not guard a hand the court had ruled procedurally unreachable. Guardianship requires access; the sealed signatory had none to give. The appointee's numbered file was proper, filed, and useless — a guardian with no ward to visit.

And the sea-as-guardian — the older instrument's claim — made the guardian the witness was the litigant. The sea had been summoned as a party, had given testimony the file could cite but never quote, and now the liturgy asked the court to seat that same party as guardian of the hand whose drowning it had just testified about. A party wearing three hats, none of them removable, each of them disqualifying the other two.

The magistrate deferred the guardianship ruling. Sine die. Until the file produced an author.

"The court," he said, standing in the nine minutes with the water at his ankles and the Crown's seal in his hand, "has a signatory it cannot serve, a guardian it cannot seat, and a case it cannot close. The file is complete in every particular except the one the file is for."

The tide turned. The nine minutes ended the way the nine minutes always end — not by clock, by water. The hearing dissolved with the hour. The clerk gathered his uncitable minutes. The advocate gathered his unanswered questions. The custody appointee gathered his numbered file.

The King had come to the waterline. He stood where the nine minutes had been, and the sea was only the sea again — no party, no litigant, no witness. Just the water, doing what the water does.

"The second tide exposed the record," he said. "The reading exposed the alteration. The cross-examination exposed the authorlessness."

He looked at the water. The heat was off him now. The pages were dry somewhere, in the registry, unbound, unsealed, kept.

"Then we find the hand that wrote before the drowning."

He walked back up the sand. The court followed. The file followed. The file always follows.

The second tide had done what the second tide does: it arrived twice, and the second arrival was the reading. The record was read. The alteration was real. The author was not on the page, not in the water, not in the grammar, not in the nine minutes.

The file needed one thing more. It was the one thing no court can summon.

A name.

The King would have to call for it. He had not called for it yet.
