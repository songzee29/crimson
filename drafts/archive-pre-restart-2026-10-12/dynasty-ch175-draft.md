# Chapter 175 — "The Recall Auction"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The catalog came by broker's courier, the way all honest thefts arrive: openly, in daylight, with a receipt.

Tessa set it on the war table between the grain accounts and the levy rolls, and the three of them — Zee, Rez, Tessa — looked at it the way you look at a sealed envelope with your name on it in someone else's handwriting.

It was a folio, bound in broker's leather, stamped with the vault rooms' mark from Khandra Parbat. Inside, page by page, the lots. Numbered by date. Each one an envelope, sealed, with a one-line description beneath it in the advocate's measured hand — the Order of the Hollow Crown's hand, the same hand that had corrected the church's confession and enclosed, with it, the correct hour, the correct chamber, the correct silence.

The nights Zee couldn't remember. Described back to him. Itemized.

Rez read the first page without touching it. His blue-white calm did the thing it always did — lowered the temperature of the room by being in it. "They kept their own watch," he said. "Through the blackouts. Closer than any confessional."

"They took notes," Tessa said. She had gone pale in the particular way of a woman whose whole trade was paper, meeting better paper. "The penitent confessed the wrong night. The Order corrected him with the right one. They were *there*, Zee. For all of them."

Zee turned the pages. Lot by lot, the dates ran backward through the blackout season like a ledger of weather. Each description was clinical, exact, unadorned — the advocate's hand did not moralize, which was worse than moralizing. The hunger had a historian. The historian was taking bids.

And at the front of the catalog, printed before Lot 1, the terms of the sale:

*A closed sale, through the brokers' channels. Bidders must prove need. Each proven bidder may demand one exhibition of any lot — the broker shall open it, read the Order's note aloud to the proven room, and reseal it with the broker's wax. The Order sells the notes. It does not sell the forgetting.*

Beneath Lot 1's description, in the broker's own smaller hand, a notation:

*Exhibited at dawn, at the church's proving. Resealed.*

The wax on Lot 1, in the catalog's little ink sketch, was already the broker's. Not the Order's.

Tessa saw him stop. "Zee."

"The opening lot," Zee said. "Its wax is already broken."

"Somebody already read it," Rez said. Flat. Not a question.

"Somebody already read it," Zee agreed, "out loud, to a room. And now the room knows the one night I can't afford to remember, and I am going to Khandra Parbat to buy the envelope it came in."

---

The vault rooms at Khandra Parbat were broker-neutral ground, and broker law was older than any crown: no blood on the floor of a broker's room. Spill it, and the market closed to every buyer at the table — the lots went back to the Order, the coin went home unspent, and the broker's doors shut for a year.

Zee sat at the long table anyway. He did not hide. The King bidding for his own nights, in front of witnesses — let the room see it. Let the file have that too.

The proven-need bidders sat around the table like a jury of appetites.

The preacher — the licensed fire-church's own, ordained in the King's hand, shepherd of the wrong-name god — sat with his hands folded and his eyes bright. He had already exhibited Lot 1 at dawn. He had what he came for. He was here the way a creditor attends a debtor's reckoning: to watch the arithmetic close.

The broadsheet's man had a printing schedule in his satchel and a pencil behind his ear, and he looked at Zee the way men look at a story that hasn't decided what it costs yet.

The magistrate's clerk had the consolidated docket's seal on his case and wanted exhibits — the King's nights, filed, stamped, entered.

And the Order's advocate sat at the far end, calm as a filed thing, bidding on every lot with the serenity of a man spending someone else's money — because he was. The Order was buying back its own notes. It would lose every lot. It didn't matter. The Order kept its own copies. The sale was never about the paper.

The broker opened the proceedings the way brokers do: by reading the terms aloud, including the one about blood on the floor, looking directly at Zee when he said it.

Lot 1 came up first.

The broadsheet's man opened the bidding before the broker finished the description. He wanted the paper to print from — he had been in the room at dawn, he said, smiling, when the wax was broken and the note was read aloud, and a printed page was worth more than a memory. "I heard it, Your Majesty," he said pleasantly. "The envelope is just the receipt."

Zee outbid him. Lot by lot, the blackout season went under the hammer, and the King bought his own hunger back in front of witnesses — the correct hours, the correct chambers, the correct silences, sealed in envelopes, paid for with the Crown's coin, in the registry's own arithmetic, where the auditors could see every line.

The preacher didn't bid past the third lot. He didn't need to. He sat with his creditor's smile and his folded hands, and when Lot 1's hammer fell to the King he inclined his head the exact depth of a man acknowledging a debt paid — as if Zee had bought the envelope *from him*.

The magistrate's clerk bid on two lots and lost both, and wrote down the winning prices in the docket's hand. Exhibits, unfiled. The file would remember the prices even without the paper.

The advocate bid on everything and won nothing, and never stopped smiling, because every bid was a filing: the Order now had, on broker's record, the proven need of a church, a broadsheet, a magistrate, and a King — cataloged in public, in order, with prices.

When the last lot fell, the broker resealed the folio and handed it across the table with both hands, the way you hand a man his own coffin nails.

Zee carried his nights home in a broker's satchel. The envelopes sat sealed in the war room like a row of small, patient coffins.

That night he opened Lot 1 alone. Rez stood by the door — not asked, not thanked, the way he stood by every door now — while Zee broke the broker's wax and unfolded the Order's note.

The correct hour. The correct chamber. The correct silence.

And the name. The Order's note named the dead — the name on the page that Zee's own mouth had never said, from the night the penitent had confessed wrong and the church had bought the wrong corpse for its sacrament.

The note was more honest than his memory. That was the worst of it. His own hunger had a better historian than he was, and he had just paid the historian's price, in full, in front of witnesses —

— for paper. He owned the paper.

Somewhere in the city, a priest was writing a sermon with the true night in it. Somewhere a broadsheet man was deciding whether the memory of a dawn exhibition was worth more sold or kept. And in the Order's archive, the advocate was filing his copies, calm as ever, because the sale had never been about the notes.

The Order sells the notes. It does not sell the forgetting.

The recall auction recalled nothing.
