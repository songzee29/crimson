# Chapter 221 — "The Lullaby's Student"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

Nine came to the nursery corridor at the hour the bells stopped counting.

He did not knock. Boys with no names do not knock — knocking is for people the house expects, and the registry had never heard of Nine. Bell, the name the dust gave him, the one the registry never heard. He stood in the corridor's lamplight the way he stood in the dust at the eighth bell: patient as weather, barefoot, holding nothing, and asked the gold man for the one thing the house had ever made that nobody had filed.

"Teach me the lullaby," Nine said. "The second verse. The one the well answered back."

Ner did not ask how he knew it. The well had gone quiet for Ner once, in the hearing of anyone standing beyond the ledger's earshot, and the house had a second verse it sang the way houses sing things — in the walls, in the water, in the grammar the file had no drawer for. Nymeris had sung it at the waterline in the ocean's own grammar, the one the tide never learned. Nine had been somewhere the night the well answered. Boys with no names stand in a lot of somewheres.

"That's not a school song," Ner said.

"It's for the school," Nine said. "The gate school. Thirty of us. None of us have names the file can hold."

The file guessed at them anyway. Ner knew the ledger — the registry kept a page per child and priced the guessing: a finder's fee for every true name delivered, the market's little bounty on the unnamed. The children were inventory-in-waiting. The file was just being patient about the paperwork.

Ner looked at the boy a long moment. The corridor was warm with the nursery's kept heat, and the gold in Ner moved the way the sea moves under a calm surface — slow, and with everything in it.

"If I teach you the verse," Ner said, "the file will hear it. The file hears everything sung by more than one mouth. It'll find a form for it."

"It finds forms for everything," Nine said. He said it without bitterness. Bitterness is for people who expected better. "They price what I don't have. They guess at my name and they take a fee for guessing. Teach me the thing they can't price."

He said his true thing then, the way he said it in the dust, the way nobody ever asked him to change: "I don't know my name."

And Ner heard what the boy was really asking — not for a song. For a true thing the file couldn't guess at for a fee. A song has no name to price. A song sung by thirty throats has no single mouth to fine.

That was the first cost. Ner felt it the way you feel a tide turn under your feet: the lullaby was the one thing the house had made that wasn't a filing. Not the boys' names — those were filed before they were born. Not the gold, not the mana, not the war table's paper. The second verse. The well's answer. The song the house made that wasn't a filing. And he was about to give it a mouth that belonged to the school.

"Come here," Ner said.

He taught it the way the house taught everything that mattered — no paper, no witness, no bell. Word by word, line by line, in the nursery corridor, in Ner's cadence, the gold of him low and warm in his throat. The second verse. The well's answer. The one the tide never learned.

Nine learned it the way children learn: wrong twice, right once, then right forever. The third time through, the boy's thin voice found the cadence — Ner's cadence, the sea's cadence, the house's — and the corridor held it the way the corridor held everything unfiled: without writing it down.

That was the second cost, and it was the worse one. Ner stood in the lamplight and heard the last unfiled thing the house had ever made living in somebody else's mouth — and he understood, the way fathers understand things they don't say aloud, that he had just taught it the way he would have taught his own boys, and there were no boys in the corridor, only a boy with no name and thirty children waiting behind him, and the teaching was the same teaching. Love doesn't check the ledger before it moves its mouth.

Nine finished the verse. The quiet after was the well-quiet, the house-quiet, the sound of something kept.

"I'll teach them at the bell," Nine said.

"I know," Ner said.

Nine went down the corridor barefoot, humming, and the humming got smaller and didn't stop.

Zee came home late, the way he came home when the paper couldn't carry the business — no warrant, no war table, Tessa's pen left behind. He found Ner in the corridor with the lamp still lit and the quiet still sitting in it.

"What was it," Zee said. Not a question, quite. The King's ear for a room.

"Nine," Ner said.

"And?"

Ner looked at his husband. The gold man and the crimson king, in the corridor where the last unfiled thing had lived for exactly as long as it took to teach it.

"I taught him the lullaby," Ner said.

Zee didn't ask why. The not-asking was older than the boys, older than the gold, older than the whole liturgy of the kept and the waiting. He put his hand on the back of Ner's neck — crimson heat, gold warmth, the marriage's true ledger — and said, "Then it's the only thing of ours they can't buy."

He didn't know yet that it was already too late for that.
