# Chapter 202 — "The Alteration's Hand"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The fragments went upstairs in padded boxes, because the sub-basement was a crime scene and the registry had a grammar for crime scenes that predated the Order, and Tessa's people worked it the way you work paper that might testify.

The tutor took one fragment. One was enough. Tide-glass is not like paper — one fragment of a sea-written pane carries the whole of the sea's argument, the way one wave carries the whole of the ocean's opinion about the shore. He worked in the annex's east reading room with the shutters closed against the harbor light, and what he did was not reading. It was *dating.* Hand first, content second. A structuralist's discipline: before you ask what a record says, you ask when the hand that wrote it was alive.

He found it in the third hour, in the barnacle-script under the waterline of the fragment's script — the alteration's own hand, the rewrite, the second line laid over the first. He read the line. Then he read the *hand*. Then he put the fragment down the way you put down a weapon you have just realized is pointed at the wrong war.

"Tessa," he said.

She was there in four minutes, because the annex had learned the tutor's tones.

"The alteration predates the registry," the tutor said. He did not preamble. Preamble was a luxury of disputes that had rules, and this one had just lost its. "Not the registry's annex. The registry. The hand that rewrote the carrier-name transaction is older than the registry's founding grammar. I have the registry's founding instruments memorized the way a priest memorizes liturgy, and this hand is older than all of them. It is older than the Order's archive. The Order did not alter this transaction. The Order could not have altered this transaction. The Order was not *invented* when this hand was writing."

The advocate — the young one — said what everyone in the room was already not saying: "Then who did?"

"The rewrite is dated," the tutor said. "Not in the registry's calendar. In the sea's. A winter. The winter Aurelia's sigil was ordered burned."

The room went quiet the way chambers went quiet — paper listening to paper. Aurelia Darkheart was the King's mother. Her sigil was a household's honor reduced to a mark, and the mark had been ordered burned in a winter, and the order had come from a hand with enough standing to order a queen's sigil burned, and now the tutor was telling them that the same winter, or the same hand, or the same standing, had rewritten a carrier-name transaction that the second tide had only just exposed — a transaction the Order had shattered fourteen panes of tide-glass to keep unreadable.

"Structural conclusion," the tutor said, and his voice had the quality of a man reading a verdict he did not want to be true. "The Order's sabotage was never about destroying the record's *contents.* The contents exonerate the Order — the Order didn't write the alteration, so the contents can't convict them. The sabotage was about destroying the record's *age.* A readable record would have proven the alteration predated the Order by centuries. An unreadable record lets the Order inherit the transaction's benefits while the household argues about a forgery nobody can date. The Order is not the author of the altered transaction." He looked at Tessa. "The Order is the alteration's *beneficiary.*"

"And the author," Tessa said, "is older than both of us, and we don't have a name."

"We have a winter," the tutor said. "And a sigil. And a King whose mother's honor was burned on somebody's order in that winter, by a hand that could also rewrite the sea's carrier-names." He paused. "I am not filing a name I do not have. I am filing the absence. The absence is evidence."

---

The margin was the second thing. The tutor found it in the fifth hour, because he was the kind of reader who read margins before text — margins being where every author, even the sea, told the truth they didn't intend to file.

The fragment's script ran to the waterline and stopped where the glass had broken. But below the waterline, in the tide-glass's own deep crust — the part of the pane the sea had grown *after* the writing, the part that was sediment rather than sentence — there was a second growth. Barnacle-script. Not testimony. A receipt.

The tutor read it three times. The third time he called Tessa back in, and his face had the expression of a man who had found the second half of a trap.

"When the second tide exposed the drowned record," he said, "it did not expose it for free. Tides don't testify pro bono. The sea took a name in exchange — a carrier-name filed under this household's seal, entered into the sea's own margin, held." He turned the fragment so the light caught the deep crust. The barnacle-script there was not a name. It was a *name-shaped absence* — a place in the sea's margin where a name had been set and then withdrawn from readability, held in the dark the way the archive's fragments were held in the dark. "The sea keeps it unpronounced. It won't show which one."

The advocate understood first, because he was young and the young understood ledgers before they understood mercy. "One of the household's filed names is drowned. Held by the sea. And we don't know whose."

"We file names constantly," the tutor said. "Heirs. Wards. Witnesses. Seals. Somewhere in the household's filings there is a name the sea now holds the margin-copy of, and the household's copy is — " He stopped. "I don't know if the household's copy is intact. A drowned carrier-name may still read on our side. It may read *wrong.* It may read as someone else's. I have no mechanism for this. I am not claiming one."

Tessa's hand went flat on the reading table. "Options."

"Two," the tutor said. "We declare the loss. We file a missing-name notice with the magistrate — the household acknowledges a carrier-name drowned and held, and petitions for the sea's margin to be produced." He let the room hear the problem. "A missing-name notice must *name the name.* We don't have it. We would be filing a notice of the loss of a name we cannot name, and the registry's grammar has no drawer for that filing. The magistrate would have to invent one."

"Or."

"Or we carry it unfiled," the tutor said. "The household proceeds with a drowned name in the sea's margin and does not declare it. The risk: a carrier-name is an instrument. It can be invoked. Whoever holds the margin-copy holds — " He stopped again. The room knew what instruments were. The room knew what *held* meant. The tutor did not finish, because finishing would have been claiming a mechanism, and he was a structuralist, and structuralists filed absences, not inventions.

"The sea can invoke one of our names," Tessa said flatly, "and we don't know which one, and we don't know what invocation costs."

"That," the tutor said, "is the price the second tide exacted. The first tide drowned the record. The second tide exposed it. And the exposure was priced in a name." He looked down at the fragment — the sea's vocabulary, the household's unknown loss, the receipt for a transaction nobody had authorized. "The household does not know which of its names is gone. The sea is in no hurry to collect."

---

Tessa filed the fragments at the sixth bell.

Not the reglassing. The opposite. The household's filing was four lines, in the registry's own grammar, and it did what the Order had not expected anyone to do with a broken archive:

*The household moves that the fourteen panes be received as filed — as broken. The vessel is the record. The shards are the testimony. No assembly is sought. No reglassing is petitioned. The record stands as the sea left it: unreadable, intact, broken.*

The advocate read it standing. "A broken vessel," he said.

"A broken vessel is still a vessel," Tessa said. "The Order's motion to compel reglassing — by the King's blood, if no other hand suffices — must now argue against *our* filing. They must move to un-file what we have filed. They must ask the magistrate to order the household to repair its own evidence." Her hand stayed flat. "Let them file that motion. Let the whole of chambers watch the Order ask the court to order a King to bleed into glass."

The Order's advocate, across the annex corridor, read the household's filing. And the Order's advocate did the coldest thing the Order had done since the sub-basement — he smiled, because the motion he had been drafting since the night of the shattering was already written, and the household's filing had just made it *necessary* instead of merely *possible.*

The motion to compel was filed at the seventh bell. The magistrate received both filings. The magistrate did not rule. The magistrate *never* ruled quickly, because the magistrate understood what the tutor understood: that this dispute was no longer about glass.

It was about whose grammar the sea would be forced to speak.

---

At the waterline, past the harbor lights, Zee stood where the second tide would come or had come — the distinction being, the tutor had begun to suspect, a filing error the sea had never corrected — and watched the water do whatever the water did. His hands were clean now. The char-ring on the reading-room floor had faded. The wing-shaped absence in his shadow was only an absence again, the way it always was when he wasn't deciding anything.

The sea kept its margin closed. The household's drowned name stayed drowned. The winter the sigil burned stayed winter. The hand older than the registry stayed old.

Somewhere below the waterline, in a pane's deep crust, a receipt sat in barnacle-script for a name nobody could read and everybody had already paid for.

The tide arrived. Or arrived twice. The sea did not say.
