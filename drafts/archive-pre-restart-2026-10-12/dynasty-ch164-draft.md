# Chapter 164 — "The Consort's Appraisal"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The registry had never refused the Order's bid.

That was the thing Zee carried into the assessment hall like a stone in his shoe: not that the bid had been accepted — it hadn't — but that it hadn't been *refused*. It sat in the clerk's tray, sealed, accruing consideration the way still water accrues depth. Every day it sat there was a day Ner Azrion was a line on the Order's offer sheet.

The assessment hall was full. That was the point of an appraisal — the law required witnesses, and the Order of the Hollow Crown had made certain the witnesses included half the court. Courtiers in their colors. Clerks with their stools. The assessor at his table with the assessment book open, its pages blank and waiting, the way a pit waits at the bottom of a staircase.

Ner stood at the center of it in his court colors, gold banked low under his skin, and looked like the only calm man in the room. Thirty-four years of never flinching. This was a room full of paper, and paper had never once been the thing that could hurt him.

Zee sat at the King's bench and did not look at him. That was the discipline: the King attended as the holder of the title, not the husband. Everyone in the hall knew it was a fiction. Fictions were what the law was made of.

The assessor rose. He was an old man, precise, with the particular gentleness of men who weigh things for a living and have learned to be kind about it, as if kindness could make the scale less of a blade.

"For the record," the assessor said, "the matter before the registry is the assessment of the surety title held on Ner Azrion, consort of the Crown, pursuant to the Crown's tax on brightness and the Order's pending offer of assumption. The registry will hear particulars, enter the valuation, and record the standing bids." He looked down at his book. "First particular. The consort's brightness."

The clerk dipped his pen.

"Pure Gold Mana," the assessor said, "of the old measure. Brightness entered as: one consort, gold-bearing, bloodline recorded." He glanced at Zee. "The King's receipt for the tax paid in full is on file and undisputed. The debt stands satisfied."

A murmur went through the hall — relief, or the performance of it.

The assessor continued. "Second particular. Lineage." He turned a page in a registry volume the size of a door. "Ner Azrion. No house. No recorded inheritance. The gold is not traced to any filed bloodline." He looked up. "The record asks: is the gold yours to be taxed?"

Every head in the hall turned to Ner.

And Ner Azrion — who had been weighed by tides and named by oceans and never once been owned by anything that wanted him — smiled the thin, cold smile he wore when a room underestimated him, and said:

"It is mine. It has always been mine. The registry taxed it, the King paid it, and I keep the receipt in my own pocket. If the law wants to know whose it is" — he looked at the assessor, then at the Order's advocate seated in the third row — "it can read the King's handwriting."

The clerk wrote it down. *The gold is his.* The words went into the book in black ink, and Zee felt the whole hall do the arithmetic: if it was his, it was taxable — and taxed it had been, paid in full, receipt issued.

Then the Order's advocate stood.

"Majesty. Magistrate. Assessor." The ledger-page face, the dry hands, the same composure as chambers. "The Order's offer stands as filed. We offer to assume the Crown's debt on the surety title — to take the title, the tax obligation, and the instrument entire into the Order's charitable holding, in forgiveness of the Crown's standing debt to the Order from the starving year." He produced the sealed bid and laid it on the clerk's stool. "The consort's value as a market instrument has a buyer. The registry need only set the price."

The hall went still. Not silent — still, the way water goes still before it decides to be ice.

Zee stood.

"The debt," Zee said, "is paid."

The advocate inclined his head. "The tax is paid, Majesty. The title is not a debt. It is an *instrument* — filed, stamped, tradable. The receipt your Majesty holds is a title of ownership, and ownership can be conveyed. The Order's offer is to purchase the instrument, not to forgive a balance."

"The instrument is not for sale."

"Then the King declines to entertain bids?" The magistrate leaned forward. "Majesty — the registry is bound to record standing offers. The appraisal must be entered regardless. That is the law."

"I know the law." Zee's voice was level, and the air in the hall went thin the way it did around him now — the tribrid stillness, dragon heat banked under the scales, the wolf watching from behind his eyes, the embers breathing at the edges of everything. Three nights since the blackout, and the hunger was coiled and patient, and every man in the hall who had read the papers knew what the last eight men who made Zee Darkheart an offer looked like afterward. "Enter it."

The assessor bent over his book. The clerk dipped his pen. And in front of the whole court, they did it — brightness, bloodline, lineage, the particulars read aloud and entered, one by one, into the assessment book, the way you enter grain into a ledger. The numbers went down in black ink. The hall listened. Nobody coughed. Nobody moved.

Ner stood through all of it without flinching. The gold stayed banked. The smile stayed thin. When the assessor read the final figure — the market price of the man Zee loved, spoken aloud in a room full of courtiers — Ner looked at Zee across the hall, and his eyes said the thing the registry could never enter into any book:

*They have a number. They don't have me.*

The magistrate struck the seal. "The appraisal is entered. The Order's bid stands recorded. The registry will consider—"

"Consider," Zee said. It wasn't a question.

"Consider, Majesty. In due course."

Zee nodded, once. He walked down from the King's bench, through the parting court, to where Ner stood at the center of the hall with his court colors and his banked gold and the whole weight of the city's arithmetic on his shoulders. Zee didn't touch him — not here, not on the record — but he stood beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and said, loudly enough for the clerk's pen to catch it:

"The title is paid in full. The receipt is in the consort's own pocket. Any man who wants to purchase what the King has already bought will find the seller unwilling — and the *goods*, advocate, unwilling too."

He looked at the Order's man. The advocate met his eyes, composed, recalculating — the same face as chambers, the same absence of fear.

And Zee smiled, and it was the smile from the harbor nights, and the whole hall felt the temperature change.

"Consider," Zee said, "in due course."

They walked out together, the King and his consort, past the clerks and the courtiers and the open assessment book with its black-ink number — and behind them, the hall exhaled, and the Order's advocate picked up his sealed bid, and did not withdraw it.

It sat in the clerk's tray. Still water, accruing depth.
