# Chapter 160 — "The Medal"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The unit came home from the border the way units came home: thinner than it left, louder than it had any right to be, and trailing the particular smell of a campaign that had gone long — smoke, horse, blood gone old in bandages.

Zee stood on the parade ground with Ner at his left and watched the Crowns' war-marshal do what war-marshals did when the levy had performed. The marshal was an old man with a face like a ledger of other people's deaths, and he moved down the line of boys and men with commendations in his hands and the Crowns' colors on his shoulders, and the whole muster stood at attention in the cold morning, and the city watched.

Zayrlen's unit. The heir's unit. The levy had taken the King's second son the way the levy took everything it was owed — by paper, by count, by the blood-oath's own arithmetic — and now the Crowns were paying it back in the only currency they minted for boys who bled well.

The war-marshal stopped in front of Zayrlen.

"Kneel," the marshal said.

Zayrlen knelt. Ten years old, in Crown colors, on the cold ground of the parade square, with the whole muster watching and the whole city beyond it. The marshal set the medal on the boy's chest — a Crown commendation, their seal, their ribbon — and the heir of House Darkheart smiled the way the dispatches said a soldier should smile, and the muster *cheered*.

Ner did not move. Zee felt it anyway — the gold under his lover's skin going very still, the way it went when Ner was holding something too heavy to set down in public.

Because they had all seen this kneel before. Not this one. The other one.

A year ago, a Veyrath captain had knelt to the boy on a different field — no colors, no ceremony, just blood recognizing blood, the captain's knee in the mud and his head bowed to the ten-year-old heir of the blood he served. That kneel had been *recognition*. This kneel was *inventory*.

And the boy — the boy was smiling.

---

The ceremony ended the way ceremonies ended: with a feast, with speeches, with the war-marshal raising his cup to the levy and the levy raising its cups back, and Zayrlen at the high table in his Crown colors with the medal on his chest, telling the table about the border in the bright, too-fast voice of a boy who had learned that the way to not cry about the dead was to talk about the living.

Zee listened. He watched his son wear the Crowns' ribbon the way other boys wore fathers' coats — proud, swallowed by it, growing into it by the hour. And he understood, with the particular cold clarity that had been visiting him more and more since the blackout, what the dread actually was.

The levy was never holding his son hostage.

It was *raising* him. And the boy was learning to be proud of it. And Zee was standing at the feast being *thanked* for it — thanked, by the war-marshal, by the muster, by the city — for lending them his son's childhood in exchange for a medal and a cheer.

He caught Ner's eye across the table. Ner looked at the medal. Looked at Zee. And in his husband's face Zee read the one sentence neither of them would say out loud at a Crown feast:

*We are losing him to the paper, and the paper is winning politely.*

---

The assassin came that night, through the service corridor, with the dead price in his pocket.

Ten thousand silver. Dead. On the boy Zee used to be — the other face of the note the creditor had carried, bought in the same gap, by a hand nobody at the renewal table had seen. The man had a knife and a route and the particular confidence of someone who had been told his target was a king who did paperwork.

He found Zee in the corridor outside his sons' rooms.

It was over in eleven seconds. The witch-mana took the man's legs out from under him before the knife cleared its sheath; the crimson threads went through his ribs the way they had gone through the creditor's; the fangs were down; and the hunger — sated on the creditor the night before, restless again, *always* restless now — fed.

Zee held the man's face while he died, the way you hold a thing you want to be sure is finished, and the blood ran hot down his chin, and the dragon heat rolled, and at the edges of everything the embers breathed, patient as ever.

Then he went through the dead man's pockets, and found the paper. Ten thousand. Dead. His own childhood price, bought and sold and carried to his sons' door.

Zee stood in the corridor with the paper in his bloody hand and the dead man at his feet, and felt the window — not a window anymore, not even a door. A *ledger*. The bounty was never a price. It was an account. And accounts didn't close when you killed the creditor. They just posted the loss and opened a new line.

He burned the paper in the unnamed fire, there in the corridor, and the ash fell gray and fine on the dead man's face.

Then he went in and checked on his sons.

Zayrlen was asleep in his Crown colors — hadn't taken them off, wouldn't, not tonight. The medal was on the bedside table, catching the candlelight. The boy's face in sleep was ten years old and utterly at peace, the way only children who have been *commended* can sleep.

Zee stood in the doorway with another man's blood still on his hands and looked at his son sleeping in the enemy's colors, proud of the medal, proud of the levy, proud of the kneel —

— and understood that the war-marshal had done to his son in one morning what eight conspirators had failed to do in seven weeks.

He had made the boy *theirs*.

Zee closed the door softly. He went and washed the blood off his hands. And in the morning, when Zayrlen came down to breakfast still wearing the medal, Zee said nothing about the colors, and nothing about the kneel, and nothing about the dead man in the service corridor —

— because the boy was smiling, and the smile was real, and that was the most terrible thing of all.

## Upcoming dark beats *(proposals only — never canon)*

1. **The Colors He Keeps.** Zayrlen is ordered to wear Crown colors at muster going forward — and comes home each night and hangs the medal beside whatever the house gives him to hang it beside. The dread: the boy keeps both kneels on the same shelf now, the Veyrath captain's and the war-marshal's, and he can't tell which one he's proud of. Neither can Zee.
2. **The Marshal's Ledger.** The war-marshal's commendation roll is filed with the registry — the heir's name, the unit's number, the campaign's count. A filed commendation is a service record, and a service record is the first page of a career. The dread: the levy didn't just decorate Zayrlen. It *enlisted* him, on paper, in front of witnesses, and the paper is already older than the boy's pride.
3. **The Account, Open.** The dead price is burned, the creditor is dead — but the bounty was an account, not a price, and accounts post losses and open new lines. The dread: somewhere a broker is already writing Zee's name into a fresh column, and the next paper won't come to the peace table. It'll come to the nursery door.
