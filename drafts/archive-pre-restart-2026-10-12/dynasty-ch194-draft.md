# Chapter 194 — "Which Tide"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The chambers session had two filings on the docket, and the magistrate took them in order, because the docket was the docket and the order was the order.

The first was the harbor's.

The slack-water dredging had pulled up a barnacled double-entry from the dead tides — the watering's whole account, kept by the second tide: every charge the first tide had ever filed, refunded line by line, in the carrier's name, priced in full moons, in a hand that wasn't the advocate's. Found property. Entered into the docket the way the docket entered everything — numbered, dated, filed.

The phenomenon was never one sea. It was two ledgers arguing.

The magistrate looked at the books — the filing tide's charges, the refunding tide's account, the carrier's name on every line of the refund — and then he looked at the Order's counsel of record for the waterline, sitting in the Order's black with his hands flat on his knees.

"Counsel," the magistrate said, "the party of record is the sea. The file holds two seas. You will declare, on the record, in chambers, which tide you represent."

The room went quiet the way chambers went quiet — paper listening to paper.

The advocate was quiet for a long moment. Tessa watched him do the arithmetic the way advocates did arithmetic — not on the tide, not on the water, on the file. The filing tide had filed the counterclaim, the barnacled refusals, the sued tide — all of it, in his hand. The refunding tide had the carrier's name on every line of its account, and the carrier's name was the one name the registry could not price.

He chose the refund.

"The Order represents the refunding tide," he said, on the record, in chambers. "The carrier's name is on every line of its account."

The magistrate wrote it down. The registry's grammar had a line for it.

*Counsel of record declares for the refunding tide.*

---

The other tide answered the way the tide answered everything.

Barnacles.

The motion arrived on the counterclaim's paper, filed in the night, in a hand that wasn't the advocate's and wasn't the registry's — the barnacled grammar, the counter-service's grammar, the deposition's grammar, sealed by nature. The first tide — the one that had filed the counterclaim, the one the advocate had just declined, on the record, in chambers — had filed a motion to disqualify its own counsel.

The sea was litigating its own representation.

The advocate read the motion twice, in chambers, the way men read paper when the paper is telling them something they didn't pay for. Then he did the one thing the Order's advocates were trained to do when the filing failed.

He objected.

"The disqualification is procedurally defective," he said. "The moving party is not a party of record. The counsel of record declares for the refunding tide. The filing tide is the same sea. A client cannot disqualify its own counsel by writing barnacles on its own counterclaim."

The magistrate heard him out. Then the magistrate stamped the disqualification. Then the magistrate stamped the advocate's objection to it. Then the magistrate did what the docket did — he filed them both, because the registry's grammar had a drawer for everything, including a sea suing its own lawyer.

"Both are entered," the magistrate said. "The disqualification and the objection. The court rules on the record as it stands: the counsel of record for the waterline is declared for the refunding tide. The filing tide's motion is entered. The filing tide proceeds without counsel of record."

Tessa's pen moved. The war-marshal's hand stayed flat.

And then the advocate — the coldest thing the Order had done in the whole paper war — stood, bowed, and filed the motion the disqualification had made possible.

"The filing tide," he said, "is a party with a split brief and no counsel of record. The Order moves that the sea's counterclaim be struck."

The room went quieter than quiet. Paper stopped listening and started holding its breath.

The magistrate heard the motion. The magistrate heard Tessa's answer — that the counterclaim was the sea's, filed, entered, argued, and a party did not lose its filing because the filing disagreed with itself. The magistrate heard the advocate's reply — that the registry's own rules struck parties that could not be represented, and the sea had just fired its lawyer in barnacles.

Then the magistrate ruled, without appetite, without mercy, the way paper rules.

"The counterclaim stands," he said. "The counsel is struck."

He set the paper down.

"The sea proceeds pro se. On its own paper. In its own grammar. The hearing will read it — four days from now, in open session — the way the file holds it: barnacled, unrepresented, and entered."

Tessa filed the ruling the way the house filed everything now. The war table read it the way the house read everything — slowly, completely, the way you read a thing that is about to ask something of you.

The Order had lost its counsel for the waterline and won the cleaner thing: an unrepresented sea at the hearing, four days out, and the advocate's hands clean of both tides.

---

The second filing on the docket came in with the marshal, and it came the way the Order always came when the paper had failed: correctly, and in wagons.

The marshal's execution report was exact. The inventory had been corrected. The repossession had gone legally clean. The marshals had walked the Veyrath kin to the wagons in the correct order. The wagons had arrived at the Order's depot —

— and the nursery's intake master had refused them.

Tessa read the report standing, because the paper didn't want a chair.

"Inventory isn't intake," she read. "The writ was for goods. The depot takes children. The paper has no drawer for the difference."

The war-marshal's hand went flat. "The kin are in the wagons."

"The kin are in the wagons," Tessa said, "between the law that took them and the law that won't receive them."

The Order's advocate was already moving — the emergency motion, filed before the ink on the marshal's report had dried, in the Order's black, with the cleanest paperwork of the season: the advocate asked the magistrate to *hold* the wagons somewhere, anywhere, because the file could not hold twelve people in a drawer, and the only somewhere the file had —

— was the gate school. The house the Order had been repossessing from.

The magistrate heard the motion. The magistrate looked at the marshal's report — *writ executed, goods refused by consignee* — and then the magistrate did the arithmetic the registry's arithmetic always did, and stamped the return the way the file stamped everything it could not place:

*Received, unfiled.*

"The wagons return to the gate school," the magistrate said, "on the Order's own motion. Received by the household. Unfiled by the registry. The inventory is the file's. The intake is no one's."

Tessa filed the ruling. The marshals filed the wagons' turn. And the inventory came home the way invoices came back when nobody signed — the catalogue numbers walking back through the gate, the Veyrath kin stepping down from the wagons in the correct order, the Order's own motion carrying them.

Rhyven stood at the gate to receive the people the Order had correctly counted. His hand was flat on the warm stone, the way it was at every hour now — listening to the road. The Specimen Seven line at his throat stood out in the light. The kin filed past him, and the gate was warm, and the road kept knocking, and the registry's stamp sat on the return the way the file stamped everything it could not hold.

*Received, unfiled.*

The Order had been right about the paper. The paper had nowhere to put them.

---

The Order's next move came at the fourth bell, in chambers, in the advocate's own hand — not a motion, not an objection, the cleanest thing the Order had filed in the whole war.

A withdrawal.

"The Order," the advocate said, "withdraws its objections to the exhibits."

The magistrate looked up. Tessa's pen stopped.

"The sealed lots," the advocate said. "The blackout-night envelopes. Chain of custody intact. Licensed seller. Paid. Filed. The Order's objections to their opening are withdrawn."

He set his hands flat on his knees, in the Order's black.

"The hearing opens them," he said. "In open session. Read aloud. Four days from now. The Order asks for nothing further in chambers."

He bowed to the magistrate — the advocate's bow, the way men filed knives — and left the room with nothing left to ask for, which was the most expensive thing a man could carry out of a room like that.

Tessa filed the withdrawal the way the house filed everything now. The war table read it the way the house read everything — slowly, completely, the way you read a thing that is about to ask something of you.

The paper war was over. The household had won the warranty and lost the seal. The Order had lost the drawer and won the nine minutes, lost its counsel and won the strike, lost the wagons and sent them home on its own motion — and now it had stopped fighting the file, because the file was working for the Order.

Three instruments sat on the war table that evening: the overseer's certification (*contents not present at opening*), the magistrate's ruling on the tides (the counterclaim stands, the counsel is struck, the sea proceeds pro se, by barnacle), and the wagons' return (*received, unfiled*).

And the hearing, four days out, would read them all aloud — and then it would open the sealed lots, and read the envelopes, and the city would learn the King's nights in the Order's grammar, with the household's stolen cadence underneath it like a watermark.

The Order didn't need the drawer anymore. It had the hour.

And the hour was sealed. And seals opened.
