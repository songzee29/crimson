# Chapter 204 — "The Unfileable Hour"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

It started over the last of the honey.

That was the thing Ner would remember later, when he tried to tell Zee why he had stood in the nursery corridor and done nothing while his sons went at each other — that it had been honey. Not an oath. Not a registry line. Not the tide's arithmetic or the Order's black or the emancipation's paper. Honey. The last spoon of it in the jar, and Zayrlen had gotten there first, and Azren had decided — with the absolute moral certainty of a small boy — that this was a crime against the house itself.

"You always get it!" Azren shouted. The gold shimmer of him was up, bright, furious — Pure Gold Mana with no grammar in it, just *feeling*, the way Ner's own mana had been before anyone taught it to be elegant.

"I got here first!" Zayrlen shouted back. His crimson-gold eyes flashed — his father's eyes, his father's heat — and the spoon went down on the nursery table hard enough to crack the glaze. "You got the last fig!"

"That was yesterday!"

"It counts!"

"It doesn't count, figs aren't honey, honey doesn't have *days*—"

"The jar has days! Look at the jar!"

They were standing on opposite sides of the nursery table, both of them up on the stools, honey on Zayrlen's chin and Azren's fist in the air, and they were loud — *loud*, the way boys are loud when nobody is watching and the noise is the whole of the point. No cadence in it. No witness. No meaning. Just two brothers being awful to each other over nothing, with everything they had.

Ner stood in the corridor doorway and felt it land in his chest like a verdict.

*Relief.*

He felt *relief*, and the dread of it was immediate and total and the exact shape of everything this house had become — that the father of the heir and the king's second son's keeper could stand in a corridor and feel grateful, *grateful*, that his boys were screaming at each other over honey, because it meant there was still one thing in this house that nobody had filed.

The tide had priced Zayrlen as a refund. The registry had emancipated him on paper. The Order had bid on his refusal-drill like it was a commodity. The magistrate had an index with a closing date, and the file had a drawer for testimony that couldn't be tried, and the whole of the paper war had been fought — every filing, every motion, every sealed envelope — over who got to write these boys down.

And here they were. Unwritten. Screaming about figs.

"You take it back!" Azren yelled. "Say figs don't count!"

"Figs count!" Zayrlen roared back, honey on his chin, crimson-gold eyes blazing — and then his stool wobbled, and Azren — mid-war, mid-shout, gold shimmer flaring — *grabbed his brother's arm to steady him*.

The fight stopped.

They stared at each other. Zayrlen, steady now, holding the table with one hand and Azren's wrist with the other. Azren, staring at the hand on his brother's arm like it had betrayed him.

"You grabbed me," Zayrlen said.

"I was winning," Azren said, which was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and Zayrlen laughed — the sharp crimson-edged laugh — and Azren laughed back, the gold shimmer of him spilling over into giggles, and then they were both laughing, honey and all, sitting down on the stools like the war had been called off by mutual treaty, and Azren pushed the jar across the table and said, "Split it," and Zayrlen said, "You take the drip," and that was that.

Ner stood in the corridor and pressed his hand to his mouth and felt the gold of him go warm and wet at the edges, and he did not go in. He let them have it — the unfileable hour, the thing the tide and the registry and the Order hadn't priced, because nobody had built it on purpose. Nobody had filed it because nobody had thought to. The alphabet had a hole, and the hole was shaped like brothers, and the brothers were sitting at the nursery table splitting honey like it was a treaty.

He heard the King before he saw him — the particular weight of Zee's step in the corridor, the air changing pressure the way it did when the Tribrid came through a room.

Zee stopped beside him. Looked through the doorway. Looked at the two boys, honey-chinned, laughing, the jar between them like a border nobody was guarding anymore.

Zayrlen looked up. Saw his father. Grinned — crimson-gold eyes bright, honey on his chin, unashamed.

"Papa," he said. "Azren says figs don't count."

"Figs count," Zee said, without missing a beat, and came into the nursery, and the air pressure of him settled the way it always settled around his people — the King's rule, but only ever *for* them, never *against* — and he picked up the jar and held it to the light like it was an exhibit, and said, very seriously, "But honey outranks figs. This is court law."

Azren — gold shimmer bright, chin up — pointed at the jar. "Then I get the drip."

"The drip is yours," the King decreed. "By law."

Zayrlen howled. Azren beamed. And Zee sat down on the third stool, too big for it, knees up, the Dragon's scales at his wrists catching the nursery lamplight, and Ner came in from the corridor and stood behind him with his hands on his shoulders, and the four of them sat in the unfileable hour like it was the only room in the house.

Later — much later, when the boys were asleep and the honey jar was washed and the corridor was quiet — Zee would say, "They fought."

"Over honey," Ner said.

"The last of it."

"They split the drip."

Zee was quiet for a moment. The fire at his edges was banked, the scales quiet, the whole of the King's terror folded down into a man on a too-small stool. "Nobody priced it," he said.

"No," Ner said.

"Not the tide. Not the registry. Not the Order."

"No."

Zee's hand found Ner's, on his shoulder. "Good," said the King who had filed everything, who had a drawer for every threat and a price for every enemy — and meant it, the way he meant everything about his own. "Let them try."
