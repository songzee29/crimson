# Chapter 176 — "The Intake's Names"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The certificates came by broker's courier, the way the commission's paperwork always came now: quietly, correctly, with a wax seal the house had never seen and a line beneath it in the nursery hand.

Nine read them in the gate school's courtyard, at the bench where the girl had bled. He read them the way the tutor had taught Zayrlen to read people — slowly, completely, the way you read a thing that is about to ask something of you.

There were eleven of them. Eleven intake children. Eleven graduation certificates, pre-signed in the nursery hand, Nine's name already on the master line — the commission's resolution to the rejected report. The children would graduate the nursery's way whether or not Nine ever taught them the leaving.

He had to sign. That was the whole of it. The master's hand, on the master line, confirming.

And every name was wrong.

Not sloppily wrong. *Systematically* wrong — the nursery hand misspelling each name the way a nursery mis-hears a frightened child, filing each one as an intake number, the names the children had answered to at the gate gone from the paper entirely. The boy who'd arrived shaking with the winter cough: intake nine-fourteen, name misspelled past recognition. The girl with the folded hands — the eight-year-old Nine had steadied through the count, blood drying on her upper lip — her certificate didn't misspell her name. It *replaced* it. A different name altogether, in the same nursery hand, as if the nursery had never asked and had simply assigned her one.

The commission never needed him to teach. It never needed him to sign. It needed his master's hand confirming the misspelling — a signature on a name the child never had.

Tessa found him at the bench with the eleven certificates spread like a losing hand. She read the first one. Then the second. Her face did the thing paper-people's faces do when the paper is lying professionally.

"They're all wrong," she said.

"They're all filed," Nine said. "Wrong is filed. Right isn't." He touched the girl's certificate — the replacement name, the stranger's name, in the nursery's neat hand. "She answered to her name at the gate for three weeks. It isn't on the paper. This is."

"And if you don't sign?"

"Then they go back as unplaced intake." He said it the way the nursery had taught him to say true things: flat, exact, without flinching. "The nursery takes them. The leaving they learned here —" he gestured at the courtyard, at the dust where thirty children had learned to walk away — "goes with them, unfiled. They graduate the nursery's way anyway. With their own names unfiled."

Tessa was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Sign them."

Nine looked up.

"Sign them," she said again, "and read each name aloud before the pen moves. Let the paper hear what it's taking."

So he did. Eleven certificates. Eleven wrong names, read aloud in the courtyard at dusk, each one spoken once, clearly, the way you speak a thing you are about to bury. Rhyven had stopped teaching; the children had gone quiet without being told. The signing became a liturgy — the master line, the nursery hand beneath it, the broker's wax waiting.

The girl's certificate he read last. The replacement name sat on the paper like a stone in a shoe. He said it aloud. It sounded like nothing. It sounded like a name for a child who had never existed, which was exactly what it was.

He signed.

When the pen lifted from the eleventh line, the courtyard exhaled — thirty children breathing out at once, the way they'd been taught, and the sound of it was the worst thing in the whole rite, because the nursery had taught them that too.

Nine stacked the certificates, sealed them for the broker's courier, and watched them go. Then he turned to Tessa and asked for the house's roll — the gate book, the one Rhyven kept in his own hand, with the names the children had answered to when they knocked in the dark.

They wrote them in. Intake number beside gate name, true name beside the paper's lie, in the house's own book, in Tessa's steady hand.

Two rolls now. The nursery held the certified lie, filed where the registry could read it. The house held the truth — eleven true names, in a book no clerk had ever seen, that the household could never show anyone.

"The toll isn't the children," Nine said quietly, looking at the two columns — the numbers matching, the names refusing to. "It's the proof they were ever somebody's."

Tessa closed the book. She didn't file it. She put it in the drawer with her ledger — the one where she kept every name, every date, every reason since Brannic Dor — and the dread sat in the room with them both, unfiled and unfileable:

Mercy had just written down the only evidence that could hang the merciful.
