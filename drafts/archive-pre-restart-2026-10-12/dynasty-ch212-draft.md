# Chapter 212 — The Absent Carrier

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

The dredge came up at the second tide, and everything about it was perfect.

The double-entry ledger rode the barge up with the seals still on it — registry wax, barnacle-crusted, unbroken. The magistrate's clerk opened it on the quay under the Order's counsel's eyes and the Crown's advocates' eyes and Zee's eyes, and every line held.

Every debit. Every date. Every tide-mark.

Except the carrier-name slot.

It was blank. Not redacted — redaction leaves a scar. Not erased — erasure leaves a smear. Blank, as though the transaction had never had a carrier, as though the whole war were being fought over a shipment that shipped itself.

The clerk turned the page. Blank there too. The carrier slot, on every entry, through every tide, for the whole altered record: empty. The drowned record had come up from the floor carrying everything it was supposed to carry except the one thing the file needed it to carry — the name of the person whose transaction had been altered.

The magistrate stared at the empty slot the way a man stares at a wound he can't remember getting.

"File it," he said finally.

The clerk reached for his pen, and stopped, because the Order's counsel was already on his feet.

"This is incomplete," the counsel said. "The record cannot be entered without the carrier. Move to strike."

"The record," the magistrate said slowly, "is the evidence. If we strike it, you have no case."

"If we enter it," the counsel said, "we have a case about nobody. The file must litigate a name that exists nowhere in its own evidence." His voice was rising, and he hated that it was rising. "Procedurally — procedurally, if I acknowledge the hole, it enters the docket. If I don't, my own filing is incomplete and the magistrate must notice — and noticing it enters it into the docket. There is no move that doesn't file the hole."

Zee stood at the back of the quay, arms folded, and watched the counsel do the one thing Zee had paid for without spending a coin: the man dismantled his own case out loud, in front of witnesses, on the record.

"Then what do you want?" the magistrate asked.

"Time," the counsel said. "Leave to produce the carrier."

"From where?" Zee asked. It was the first thing he'd said all tide, and every head turned. "You want leave to produce a name that exists nowhere. From what source? The drowned record is the record. The record is blank. There is no other record — if there were, you'd have filed it, and your case wouldn't depend on dredging up a floor that was already somebody else's before you arrived."

The counsel's mouth worked. He had nothing. The blank slot had taken everything: the bribe money, the crews, the charter, the writ — all of it spent to bring up a file that testified against its own existence.

The magistrate nodded to the clerk. The clerk, with the careful hands of a man burying something, entered the finding.

Finding: the carrier-name slot is blank across the altered transaction record. Cause: unknown. Name: not produced. Not producible from the evidence.

The archive filed it. In duplicate. Two copies of nothing, stamped and dated, because procedure is procedure and the file keeps what the file keeps.

That night, one of the Order's men tried to take the ledgers off the barge by force — rope knife, dark water, no witnesses. There was one witness. There was always one witness, because the king had stopped investigating and started arriving.

The man died quietly, over the rail, and the water took him without a ripple worth the name. The ledgers never left the barge. Zee's rule had never been simpler: threats die. The man had threatened the nothing the file was built on, and the nothing stayed intact, and the man did not.

In the morning the counsel looked at the two stamped copies of the finding of nothing and understood the arithmetic the whole war had been building toward: he had paid for a dredge, paid for crews, paid for a charter, paid for the hour — and what he had purchased, at the end of it, was the Crown's own archive confirming, twice, that his case had no name in it.

You cannot litigate a ghost. You can only pay for the finding, file it in duplicate, and let the court keep the receipts.
