# Chapter 196 — "The Engineer's Last Room"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

The fortress sank the way boats sink — slowly, then in pieces, then all at once.

The eastern curtain went first. The bedrock under it ran like wax from a candle held too close, and the wall leaned past the angle where leaning became falling, and fell — not outward, not inward, *down*, into the earth that had stopped being earth. The moat drained into the hollow in a single choking gasp, the chains snapping like bowstrings. The gatehouse, with its murder corridor and its flood and its starving and its drop, folded at the knees and sat down in the karst, and the men on the wall were no longer on the wall — they were running down a slope of their own fortress, and the slope was still moving.

The fire Zee had put into the ground did not go out. It kept its own schedule. It worked the shelf the way the shelf worked itself — downhill, toward the hollow, toward the tower.

Thoss's men broke. That was the mercy of it, if mercy was the word for men running from a king they could not reach with arrows. Zee let them run. His rule was a structure too, and it had its own unbreachable parts: the men who had only held walls were not the men who had opened the gate to Varren. They ran into the dusk with the fortress sinking behind them, and Zee stood at the curtain's foot with the glow climbing his forearms, and did not follow them.

His eye was on the tower.

The engineer's tower had been built by the engineer. It was going to go down last, because everything Thoss had ever built went down last — that was the design, the whole of his philosophy in stone: the core held when the walls failed. The core held, and the core held, and the tower leaned into the sink with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and then the foundation under it gave way and the tower rode the ground down forty feet into the dark, intact.

Inside, in the engineer's room — stone four feet thick on every side, one door, no windows — Vaeren Thoss lit his second lamp.

The room was supposed to be unbreachable. The room had performed. The walls were unbroken. The door was unbroken. The floor —

The floor was gone.

Thoss stood in the center of his unbreachable room with a lamp in each hand and looked at the floor that wasn't there, at the dark below where the bedrock had been, at the heat coming up out of it like breath. His wall charts were gone. His maps were gone. The chair he had designed for a man who thought was gone. Everything he had engineered, everything he had measured and mortared and believed in, stood around him in four-foot walls of perfect, intact, useless stone.

The door was above him now. The tower had settled nose-down in the hollow, and the door was up a slope of broken masonry, and through the doorway, stepping down the slope like a man descending stairs, came the king.

Zee's hands were still lit at the knuckles with the color of forge-iron. His eyes were the tribrid's eyes — the Witch's seeing, the Vampire's patience, the Wolf's certainty — and behind them, in the dark of him, the Dragon blood was up, and beneath all of it the burning that had no name burned, the way it always burned: unshown, unspoken, and entirely present.

"You housed him," Zee said.

Thoss set both lamps down on the nothing where his desk had been. His hands were shaking. He was an old man in a room he had built to outlive him, and the room had outlived its floor, and the arithmetic of his whole life was being done in front of him by someone who hadn't brought a single siege engine.

"I housed many men," Thoss said. "I am a builder. Men need roofs."

"Varren planned the jump from inside your walls." Zee came down the slope a step. The heat rose between them like a third man in the room. "My men died on a road your gate opened. Don't make me investigate you. My rule is simpler than your walls were."

Thoss looked at him — really looked, the way engineers looked at failures — and something in the old man's face did the last calculation it would ever do. Not a plea. A recognition.

"The walls," Thoss said, "were never the problem."

"No," Zee said. "They were the distraction."

He crossed the room. He did it the way the Wolf crossed rooms — no hurry, no waste, the distance disappearing under him like the bedrock had — and Thoss did not run, because there was nowhere in four feet of stone to run to, and Zee took the old man's throat in one hand.

The Vampire in him felt the pulse. The Wolf in him felt the stillness underneath the pulse, the old man's body doing what bodies did when they understood. Zee's other hand went to Thoss's chest, over the heart, and the fire that had no name ran down his arm and into the engineer the way it had run into the stone — downward, structural, looking for the foundation.

Thoss's eyes found Zee's. There was no begging in them. There was, at the very end, the engineer's honesty.

"How—" Thoss got out, "—how long—"

"Forty feet," Zee said. "You built on forty feet and called it ground."

The fire reached the heart. It did not burn the man the way fire burned men. It burned the structure of him — the held breath, the held posture, the held life — the way it had burned the shelf. Vaeren Thoss's heart stopped the way walls fell: slowly, then in pieces, then all at once.

Zee laid him down on the floor that wasn't there. He closed the old man's eyes with two fingers, because the dead were owed the small courtesies even when the living weren't owed investigations.

Then he climbed out of the engineer's last room, up the slope of broken masonry, out of the tower's drowned throat, and stood on the rim of the hollow and watched the fortress of Vaeren Thoss settle into the earth it had been standing on — stone by stone, wall by wall, the boast made of stone becoming a geography lesson.

Behind him, a half-league back, Rez watched the riders watch the king, and said nothing, because guardians said nothing when the work was done.

The war table struck the name that night. Three names down — Varren, Dor, Thoss — and the list the king carried had five names left on it, and every fortress in the province had just learned the same lesson the engineer's room had learned, and the men who built walls were already asking each other the question no mason could answer.

*If the ground is the floor — who do you hire to build that?*
