# Chapter 223 — The Gap, Priced

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves.**

---

The magistrate certified the defect before the market knew what to call it.

That was the error — not the nine minutes themselves; the nine minutes had been there since the first tide-table, hiding inside the Order's tide-turns the way a man's knife hides inside his sleeve, doing nothing, waiting to be drawn. The error was the certification. The Tide's Auditor had measured the table against the actual sea and found it nine minutes wrong, and the magistrate, doing what magistrates do when the arithmetic is filed in triplicate and signed by both parties, had ruled Crown time law and the Order's tide-table nine minutes wrong. Filed. Stamped. Crown paper.

I read the certification the morning it was published. I read it three times. Then I went to the brokers' hall and watched the arithmetic do what arithmetic always does when it finds an unowned space with a price on it.

Nine minutes per tide-turn. Legally unowned time — not Order time, not Crown time, a strip between two clocks where neither filing instrument had jurisdiction because both instruments disagreed on where the strip was. And the magistrate's certification said so. In writing. On Crown paper. The defect had a deed now.

The brokers opened the market before noon.

They priced it by the hour. Nine minutes doesn't sound like an hour, but the brokers sell in standard instruments — a full tide-turn's gap, forty-five minutes across five turns per day, a workday's worth of legally unowned time, traded in parcels, sold to parties who need their paper to exist when the file says it didn't. That is the whole of the instrument. That is all it is. A backdated signature. A receipt for a meeting that happened in a hour the courts don't own. A contract executed at a tide-turn that the Crown's clock never saw.

The Order's advocate was at the brokers' table before the first parcel sold.

I was there. I stood in the gallery of the brokers' hall the way I stand everywhere now — where the exits are, where the ledgers are, where the clerks keep the duplicate copies — and I watched the enemy do what it always does with its own wreckage. It leased gap-filing to the League's delivery contracts.

The League delivers things. That is the whole of the League's business, and the deliveries are the kind that arrive sealed and leave wrapped in canvas and are never discussed at dinner. The League had a problem: the League's contracts were starting to be examined. The King's incident file owned six of the Order's dead, and the Crown's investigators had begun asking when, exactly, deliveries happened, because the tide-table was the alibi and the tide-table was now certified wrong and every alibi in the city was being re-checked against Crown time. A delivery that arrived at 2:00 on the Order's table arrived at 2:09 on the Crown's clock, and the nine minutes between were the difference between an alibi and a confession.

The gap solved it.

A killing filed in the nine minutes isn't in either time. The Crown's clock says it didn't happen when the Crown was watching. The Order's table says it didn't happen when the Order was watching. The file — the same file that certified the defect, the Crown paper that deeded the gap — says the gap exists, and the gap is legally unowned, and a filing executed inside unowned time is a filing the docket cannot attach to a clock it recognizes.

The first gap-filing went to the League that afternoon. A courier. Nobody important — the kind of man the city pays to carry sealed things and then stops remembering. He had carried one sealed thing too many and the League's counterparty decided the carrier was the message. The League's broker filed the contract in the gap: delivery to be executed between the clocks, at the tide-turn, in the nine minutes, receipt unfileable, witnesses inadmissible.

The courier was found at the low tide, in the water, nine minutes before the Crown's clock said the tide turned. The water kept him. The file kept the filing. The magistrate's investigators read the filing and found a contract that existed in time the Crown didn't own, for a death the Crown's clock couldn't place.

The King read the filing. He read the certification. He read the market's price list — nine minutes per tide-turn, priced by the hour, forty-five minutes a day of legally unowned time, the League's first contract already executed inside it.

Then he did what he does now. He put it in writing. In triplicate. With the Order's own certification quoted back at it.

The filing was entered before the evening bell, in the magistrate's chamber, in the King's counsel's hand, and I read it the way I read everything the King files — standing at the clerk's shoulder, where I can see the ink dry.

It said this: *The Order certified the defect. The defect is Crown paper. Crown paper is Crown property. The nine minutes per tide-turn, being defect so certified, are hereby claimed by the Crown as Crown time — the Crown's own unowned hours, held against the docket's balance, administered under the magistrate's seal.*

The Order's certification, quoted word for word, page and line. The advocate's own signature on the certificate. The magistrate's own stamp.

The gap was no longer unowned. The gap was the King's.

And the dread — the coldest arithmetic I had priced all year — was that the King was right. The file said so. The Order had spent years building instruments out of the Crown's own paper, and the King had just built one out of the Order's. The defect kept the docket from balancing. The defect was Crown paper. And Crown paper, when the King decides to collect on it, is collected.

The brokers' hall went quiet the way a room goes quiet when somebody reads a will aloud and somebody else is the executor. The League's broker folded his price list very slowly. The Order's advocate stood at the railing and said nothing, because there was nothing to say — his own certification was on the King's filing, quoted back at him, in triplicate, and the nine minutes had just changed hands without a single coin moving.

The gap was open. The gap was the King's. And the market — the beautiful, terrible, honest market the brokers had opened that morning — was now trading in Crown property without a license.

That was the evening bell. The filing was in. The triplicate was filed. The Order's words were the King's words now, and the King was going to charge the enemy rent on its own nine minutes.

Nobody in the brokers' hall was smiling. Least of all me. Because I had already priced what the rent would cost, and I knew who was going to have to pay it, and it wasn't the brokers, and it wasn't the Order, and it wasn't the League.

It was going to be the King.
