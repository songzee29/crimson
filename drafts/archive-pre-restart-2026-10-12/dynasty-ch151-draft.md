# Chapter 151 — "Jorren Vale"

**DRAFT — PROPOSAL ONLY. Not canon until Zee approves. Reviewed by Vigil next shift.**

---

Jorren Vale did not run, and did not fight, and did not hide.

That was what made him different from the other seven, and Zee knew it before he ever saw the man's house — because runners leave heat and fighters leave noise, and Jorren Vale had left nothing at all for three weeks except a silence so complete it had to be deliberate.

The townhouse was in the Garden District, behind walls covered in jasmine that had no business blooming in October. Old money. Older than the eight, older than the harbor, older probably than the idea of hiring men to jump a king. Zee stood across the street in the rain and looked at the lit windows and felt the wards — not his, not witch-mana, something bought and paid for, humming faint against his teeth like a bad filling.

*He knows I'm here,* Zee thought. *He's known for weeks. "Jorren waits."*

The hunger was awake and walking beside him now, no longer something that woke him but something that *accompanied* — a second pulse under his own, sated and singing since Sera, worse sated than starving. The wolf prowled its edges. The dragon heat moved through his scales like weather. And at the edges of all of it, the embers breathed.

*Six,* he thought. *Almost.*

He walked through the gate. The bought wards shivered and parted around him like curtains — they were built to keep out thieves and spirits, not kings — and the jasmine shook rain off its blooms, and the front door opened before he reached it.

"Zee Darkheart." The man in the doorway was in his early sixties, silver at the temples, a glass of something amber in his hand that he wasn't drinking. "I told them you'd come on a rainy night. It seemed right." He stepped back. "Come in. I've kept the evening free."

---

The study was wood and leather and the particular quiet of rooms where expensive decisions get made. A fire burned in the grate — real fire, orange and ordinary, and Zee felt his own fire shift under his skin at the sight of it, interested, proprietary.

"Sit," Vale said, gesturing at the chair across from his desk. "Please."

Zee didn't sit. He stood in the middle of the rug with rain dripping off his coat and looked at the paymaster — the broker, the man who'd sold the highest bidder a demonstration, eight men and one harbor and one unkillable king.

"You didn't run," Zee said.

"Vane ran." Vale set down his glass. His hand was steady — Zee gave him that, the way he'd given Vane credit for the hand. "Vane ran first class with a false name and nine days of hope, and you let him have every minute of it because you wanted him to choose the room. I read Sera's copies, you know. Before she died. She sent me one set, in the end — the planner's courtesy." A thin smile. "So no. I didn't run. Running was Vane's mistake, and I don't repeat mistakes I've already paid for."

"Thoss fought."

"Thoss was a blade. Blades fight." Vale leaned back in his chair, and for all the calm, Zee could see the calculation running behind his eyes — the broker's mind, still brokering, even now. "I'm not a blade, Zee. I never held a knife. I held a *ledger*. There's a difference, and I think you know it, because you let the accountant live."

"Neris Pell held a pen." Zee's voice was level. "You held the men."

"I brokered a demonstration." Vale spread his hands. "Let me tell you what that means, since you're here and the night is young and we both know how it ends. A demonstration is not an assassination. You can't be killed — everyone in this business has known that since you were *born*, boy. Stakes, magic, venom, death itself — nothing takes. So no one pays for your death. They pay to *watch*." He leaned forward. "Eight men, one harbor, one unkillable king. The bidder didn't buy a kill. The bidder bought a *viewing*."

The hunger shifted under Zee's ribs. *Testing, not killing.* Thoss's list. Vane's claim — *funded* — sitting in his head like a hot coal, unconfirmed. Sera's sealed answer, burned with the copies.

"Who was the bidder?" Zee asked quietly.

Vale smiled, and it was almost sad. "Ask your father."

The fire popped in the grate. Rain hit the windows. The shadows on the study wall were too large, and they moved half a breath out of sync.

"That's not an answer," Zee said.

"It's the only one I have left to sell." Vale stood, slowly, and came around the desk — no weapon, no ward-trigger, just a sixty-year-old broker in a good suit walking toward the thing he'd been waiting three weeks for. "Here's my last play, Zee, and it's an honest one: everything I have. The network, the money, the names of every man who's ever bought a demonstration. Yours, for the taking. Work with me — not for me, *with* me — and I'll help you end your father's test instead of just surviving it."

Zee looked at him for a long moment. The crimson-black horns caught the firelight. The scales had come up his throat like a tide of dark glass.

"You brokered the eight," Zee said.

"I did."

"You sold men a viewing of my death."

"I did."

"Selk Varren built it. Brannic Dor swung it. Thoss held the knife. Vane paid for it. Sera planned it." Zee took a step closer, and the air in the study bent faintly around him, the way air bends around a fire. "And you sold the tickets."

Vale didn't step back. "Yes."

"Then you know how this ends."

"I've known since the harbor." Vale's voice didn't waver. "I just wanted to make the offer first. A broker always makes the offer."

Zee put his hand on Jorren Vale's chest.

And the fire rose.
