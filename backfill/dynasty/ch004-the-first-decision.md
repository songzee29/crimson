Chapter Four

The First Decision

The first arrow landed before dawn.

It struck the outer wall of Castle Darkheart and shattered against stone.

No soldier died.

No gate broke.

But the message tied beneath the arrow survived.

Captain Vale carried it personally to the throne room.

King Victor read it once.

Then again.

Queen Aurelia stood several paces away, Zee in her arms.

The crimson hatchling rested against her side beneath the folds of a heavy cloak, hidden from the growing number of foreign eyes beyond the walls.

Victor handed the message to no one.

Aurelia watched his face.

“What does it say?”

He folded the parchment.

“Nothing new.”

“Then read it.”

Victor looked at her.

Aurelia did not move.

Finally he spoke.

“They demand Zee be brought beyond the eastern gate before midday.”

“And if we refuse?”

“They begin the siege.”

The queen’s expression turned cold.

“Then let them begin.”

Several ministers shifted uneasily.

Victor said nothing.

Aurelia looked toward the assembled council.

“You heard me.”

The High Marshal lowered his head.

“My Queen.”

“We do not surrender children to armies.”

“No,” Victor said.

Every eye turned toward him.

He stepped down from the throne.

“We do not.”

Aurelia studied him carefully.

For one moment, she heard the man from before the prophecy.

Then Victor continued.

“Not because they are wrong to fear him.”

The room chilled.

Aurelia’s face hardened.

“Victor.”

“But because no foreign crown decides what happens inside House Darkheart.”

The distinction was unmistakable now.

The council heard it.

Captain Vale heard it.

Aurelia heard it most of all.

“You speak of our son as though he were property.”

“I speak as a king.”

“You used to speak as his father.”

Victor’s jaw tightened.

“This is not the place.”

“No. The place was our nursery, and you stopped speaking there too.”

The hatchling stirred beneath her cloak.

Zee’s Crimson Mana brightened faintly.

Victor noticed.

Of course he noticed.

Now he noticed everything.

Every flicker.

Every change in Zee’s breathing.

Every strange movement of the dragon beside him.

He collected details as if building a case against his own child.

Aurelia saw that too.

Fear turned slowly into anger.

“You will not touch him.”

The council went completely silent.

Victor’s eyes met hers.

“I have not said I would.”

“You no longer need to.”

Before he could answer, the fortress shook.

A deep impact thundered against the eastern wall.

Then another.

Siege stones.

The war had begun.

—

The first assault lasted three hours.

House Darkheart’s witches stood upon the battlements and cast through carved defensive channels built into the ancient walls. Violet force struck ladders from stone. Blue-white frost from enchanted siege wards froze ropes and shattered wooden towers. Green fire spilled across an advancing ram.

None of it carried Crimson Mana.

That belonged to Zee alone.

Darkheart soldiers filled the air with arrows.

Boiling oil rained from murder holes.

Foreign troops screamed beneath the walls while their kings remained safely beyond range.

The castle held.

But House Darkheart paid for it.

By midday, bodies filled the eastern courtyard.

Some belonged to invaders.

Many did not.

A healer died with both hands inside a soldier’s open abdomen.

A tower collapsed after repeated strikes and buried seventeen archers.

A captain Victor had known since boyhood bled out against the inner gate.

War did what fear always promised.

It made everything simpler by making everything worse.

Victor walked among the dead.

Blood covered his boots.

A young soldier grabbed his cloak.

“My King.”

Victor crouched.

The soldier could not have been older than eighteen.

“Did we hold?”

“Yes.”

The boy smiled.

Then died.

Victor remained there for several seconds.

Behind him, another siege stone struck the wall.

The prophecy whispered.

The world shall break before it teaches him how to die.

Victor looked toward the castle tower where Zee slept.

For the first time, resentment entered his fear.

Not hatred of the child.

Hatred of what followed him.

That distinction would not survive forever.

—

In the nursery, Aurelia heard the war through stone.

Every impact made dust fall from the ceiling.

Every horn sent the crimson hatchling into alert stillness.

Zee had begun crying.

Not loudly.

But enough to make Aurelia’s heart ache.

She held him close.

“I know.”

Another strike shook the room.

“I know.”

The hatchling crawled into her lap and pressed his head against Zee.

Fear moved between them.

Then comfort.

Their strange link did not give either child knowledge.

Only presence.

Neither was alone.

The eldest royal witch entered with blood across her sleeve.

Aurelia looked up.

“Are the walls holding?”

“For now.”

“How many dead?”

The witch hesitated.

“Too many.”

Aurelia closed her eyes.

The woman approached Zee.

Crimson Mana drifted around him more strongly than before.

“His power responds when the castle is struck.”

Aurelia’s eyes opened.

“Do not call it power as if he commands it.”

“I know he does not.”

“Then say what you mean.”

The witch nodded.

“His mana becomes more active when he is distressed.”

Aurelia looked down at him.

“Like any child.”

“Perhaps.”

The uncertainty mattered.

The witch did not pretend to understand.

She had learned better.

Aurelia asked, “What happened to your magic during his birth?”

The woman considered carefully.

“It remained inside me.”

“But?”

“I could not reach it.”

“Because of Zee?”

“I do not know.”

“You felt his mana.”

“Yes.”

“Was it stronger?”

The witch looked toward the infant.

“Overwhelming.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No.”

Aurelia appreciated the honesty.

The witch continued.

“I have spent my life learning the shape of my own magic. That night, I knew it was still there. I simply could not hear myself beneath him.”

“Beneath my newborn son.”

“Yes.”

Aurelia looked toward the window.

Outside, men were killing one another because everyone believed observation had already become understanding.

It had not.

No one knew what Zee was capable of.

No one knew what he would become.

No one knew whether the Blood Rain had been caused by him, announced him, reacted to him, or merely occurred beside him.

Yet armies had come anyway.

Fear had filled every unanswered question with the worst possible answer.

Aurelia whispered, “Victor is doing the same thing.”

The witch did not respond.

She did not need to.

—

That evening, a second message came beneath a white flag.

The foreign coalition requested a single representative.

Victor allowed one envoy inside the outer gate.

The man entered without armor.

He was elderly, gray-bearded, and exhausted.

Victor met him in the lower audience hall rather than the throne room.

“You have lost thousands today,” the envoy said.

“So have you.”

“We can lose more.”

“So can we.”

The man looked at Victor for a long moment.

“Is that what you want?”

“No.”

“Then end it.”

Victor’s expression hardened.

“You know our terms.”

“You have no terms. You have a demand.”

“We have terror.”

“That is not law.”

“No. It is stronger.”

Victor almost drew his sword.

The envoy raised both hands.

“I am not your enemy, Victor.”

“You arrived with an army.”

“I arrived because my king watched blood fall from the sky.”

“So did mine.”

“Wolves slaughtered villages.”

“I know.”

“Witches became helpless.”

“They recovered.”

“Vampires screamed about a heartbeat that was not theirs.”

Victor said nothing.

The envoy stepped closer.

“And every report leads here.”

“To a child.”

“To something no one understands.”

Victor’s eyes sharpened.

“Then perhaps none of you should pretend you know enough to kill him.”

The envoy nodded slowly.

“Perhaps.”

Victor had not expected agreement.

“But perhaps you do not know enough to let him live.”

That landed.

The man saw it.

“You are his father.”

Victor’s jaw tightened.

“You think I cannot see it?”

The envoy lowered his voice.

“I think you are more frightened than any king outside these walls.”

Victor stared at him.

“Leave.”

“Victor—”

“Leave before your white flag becomes your burial cloth.”

The envoy bowed.

At the door, he stopped.

“If you refuse us, the assault resumes at dawn.”

Victor did not turn.

The door closed.

He remained alone.

More frightened than any king outside these walls.

The insult should have angered him.

Instead, it felt true.

—

That night, Victor summoned Captain Vale.

No council.

No ministers.

No Aurelia.

Only the two of them inside the king’s private chamber.

Victor poured wine but did not drink it.

“How many guards outside the nursery?”

“Eight.”

“Double them.”

Captain Vale hesitated.

“Against what?”

Victor looked at him.

“Everyone.”

“Foreign assassins?”

“Yes.”

“Court factions?”

“Yes.”

Vale waited.

“And the queen?”

Victor said nothing.

The captain’s expression changed.

“My King.”

“No one removes Zee from that wing without my command.”

“Not even Queen Aurelia?”

Victor’s eyes hardened.

“I said no one.”

Vale breathed slowly.

“You understand what that order means.”

“Yes.”

“Do you?”

Victor stepped toward him.

“I will not have twelve kings tear apart my kingdom deciding what should happen to my son.”

“Then what will happen?”

“I decide.”

Vale stared at him.

For years, he had followed Victor through wars without hesitation.

This frightened him more than battle.

“You intend to kill the prince?”

Victor’s face tightened.

“No.”

The answer came immediately.

Too immediately.

Then he looked away.

“Not tonight.”

Captain Vale went still.

Victor heard his own words.

There was no taking them back.

The king walked toward the window.

Far beyond the walls, enemy fires covered the plain.

“If the prophecy is true, attacking him may make him worse.”

“May.”

“Yes.”

“And if it is false?”

Victor looked toward him.

“Then a child dies.”

The words nearly broke whatever remained of the father inside him.

Vale spoke carefully.

“That should matter.”

“It does.”

“Then stop.”

Victor closed his eyes.

He wanted to.

Aurelia’s voice echoed through his memory.

You held him yesterday.

He remembered Zee sleeping against him.

His son.

His heir.

Then another siege stone struck the outer wall.

The room trembled.

Victor opened his eyes.

“And if I stop and every kingdom burns because I was too weak to act?”

“You do not know that will happen.”

“I do not know that it will not.”

Vale shook his head.

“That is fear speaking.”

“Yes.”

Victor turned toward him.

“And kings who ignore fear bury kingdoms.”

He walked to a locked cabinet and removed a small iron key.

Captain Vale stared at it.

“What does that open?”

“The old execution chamber beneath the western tower.”

Vale’s face went pale.

Victor placed the key on the table between them.

“I am not ordering his death.”

“Then why prepare the chamber?”

Victor looked at the key.

“Because if I decide the prophecy leaves me no choice… I will not make that decision with twelve foreign armies watching.”

The captain said nothing.

Victor’s voice became colder.

“No foreign king will claim they killed a Darkheart prince.”

“No priest will parade his body.”

“No vampire will take his blood.”

“No witch will dissect what remains.”

“If Zee dies, it will happen inside these walls.”

Vale whispered, “By your hand.”

Victor did not answer.

That was answer enough.

—

Near midnight, Aurelia woke and found Victor standing beside the cradle.

She sat upright.

“What are you doing?”

Victor looked toward her.

“Watching him.”

The crimson hatchling was awake.

His body pressed against Zee.

Teal-and-gold mana trembled faintly across his scales.

Danger.

Zee shifted in his sleep.

Victor reached toward him.

The hatchling hissed.

Aurelia stood.

“Do not.”

Victor stopped.

The distance between his hand and his son was less than an inch.

For several heartbeats, no one moved.

Then Zee opened his crimson eyes.

He saw Victor.

His tiny hand lifted.

The same gesture as before.

An invitation without understanding.

Victor stared at it.

Every part of him that remained a father wanted to take that hand.

He almost did.

Then the prophecy returned.

The dead soldier in the courtyard.

The armies outside.

The Blood Rain.

The wolves.

The impossible heartbeat.

Victor lowered his hand.

Aurelia’s face changed.

She understood.

Not everything.

Enough.

“You decided.”

Victor looked at her.

“I decided nothing.”

“You prepared something.”

His silence betrayed him.

Aurelia moved between him and the cradle.

“If you ever come near him with death in your heart, you will have to go through me.”

Victor’s expression hardened.

“You are threatening your king.”

“I am warning my husband.”

The hatchling curled tighter around Zee.

Victor stared at his family.

His wife.

His son.

The impossible dragon who had appeared beside him.

For one brief moment, grief almost conquered fear.

Almost.

Then another war horn sounded beyond the walls.

Victor stepped backward.

“No foreign ruler determines what happens to Zee.”

Aurelia’s voice was quiet.

“And neither does a frightened father.”

Victor left without answering.

He walked through the sleeping castle until he reached the western tower.

Below it, servants had already begun clearing dust from the old execution chamber.

Iron chains were removed from the walls.

A stone table was washed.

Torches were lit.

Victor stood at the top of the stairs and watched.

He had not yet ordered Zee brought there.

He had not yet raised a weapon against him.

He had not yet crossed the final distance between fear and murder.

But he had prepared the place where he might.

Outside, twelve kingdoms waited for Castle Darkheart to surrender the First Tribrid.

Inside, Queen Aurelia held her son and understood that the walls protecting Zee had become another kind of danger.

And before any foreign king could reach the newborn prince, the first man to decide Zee might have to die had already been crowned long before Zee was born.

His father.
