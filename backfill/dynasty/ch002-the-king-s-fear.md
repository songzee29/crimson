Chapter Two

The King’s Fear

Castle Darkheart did not sleep.

The Blood Rain ended before dawn, but crimson water continued to run from the roofs and battlements. It gathered in the mouths of stone beasts, flowed through the courtyards, and spilled down the mountain in narrow red streams. Servants scrubbed the floors without making them clean. Priests burned incense in every hall. Soldiers stood along the walls with bows strung and swords loosened in their sheaths.

No enemy had reached the kingdom.

Fear had entered first.

Inside the royal chamber, the physicians completed their work around Queen Aurelia while the witches tested their recovered magic.

Violet light moved across torn flesh and eased the queen’s pain. Blue sigils appeared above bowls of water, purifying the blood from their surfaces. Pale green wards settled across the doors and windows.

Every spell answered normally.

That frightened the witches almost as much as their earlier helplessness.

The eldest royal witch stood near the window, watching the last traces of blood drip from the glass.

“My power was inside me the entire time,” she said.

The younger witch beside her nodded. “I could feel it. I simply could not make it answer.”

“Nor could I.”

“Was it the child?”

The elder looked toward Zee.

He slept against Aurelia’s chest. The crimson hatchling remained pressed along his side, its tail curled protectively around the blanket. Their breathing had fallen into the same slow rhythm.

“I do not know.”

“You felt the pressure.”

“I felt many things tonight. Certainty was not among them.”

The younger witch lowered her voice. “He has the blood hunger.”

The elder’s eyes narrowed.

“You have no proof of hunger.”

“He has fangs.”

“So do creatures that have never tasted blood.”

“And the sound inside him?”

The elder glanced toward the door, ensuring Victor remained too distant to hear.

“The royal wolf guard heard it.”

“He said the prince carried the scent of wolf blood.”

“A frightened man said many things.”

“Do you believe he was wrong?”

The elder did not answer.

House Darkheart had ruled as an ancient Witch Kingdom for generations. Its royal line was known for powerful witches, long-lived blood, and rulers whose magic had broken enemy armies.

No Darkheart child had ever been born with vampire fangs.

No newborn prince had ever carried the scent of a wolf.

No witch had ever produced mana like the crimson force surrounding Zee.

The younger woman whispered, “Three natures.”

“Do not name what you do not understand.”

“Witch. Vampire. Wolf.”

The elder’s gaze sharpened.

“Enough.”

But the words had already entered the room.

Three natures.

A trinity of blood no kingdom had ever witnessed.

A First Tribrid.

The name would not remain inside Castle Darkheart for long.

Near the bed, Victor watched Zee sleep.

He had dismissed most of the attendants and refused every request to remove the dragon hatchling. Twice, guards offered to place the creature inside an iron cage. Twice, Zee’s Crimson Mana surged before any hand reached it.

Victor had ordered them to stop trying.

He told himself the command protected Aurelia from another disturbance.

The truth was more difficult.

The hatchling had not threatened Zee.

It had calmed him.

Each time the child stirred, the dragon moved closer. Each time the hatchling made a weak sound of hunger or discomfort, Zee’s tiny fingers found its scales.

Victor understood nothing about the connection.

He could still see the way Zee had held his finger.

His son’s grip had been too strong for a newborn, yet the memory did not frighten him as much as it should have.

Not yet.

Aurelia opened her eyes.

“You are staring.”

Victor looked at her. “You should sleep.”

“So should you.”

“I have a kingdom to command.”

“You have a son.”

The words stopped him.

Aurelia shifted Zee carefully into her arms and held him toward Victor.

“Take him.”

Victor did not move.

The crimson hatchling lifted its head.

“It watches me,” Victor said.

“It watches everyone.”

“It does not watch you that way.”

“Perhaps it has good judgment.”

Despite the night’s horror, Victor almost smiled.

Aurelia extended Zee farther.

“Hold your son.”

Victor sheathed his sword and approached.

He slid one hand beneath Zee’s head and the other beneath his body. The child felt impossibly small against him.

Zee opened his crimson eyes.

Victor’s breath caught.

No infant should have looked so still.

So aware.

Yet there was no ancient intelligence inside the child’s gaze. No hidden message. Zee was only a newborn trying to understand warmth, sound, hunger, and the unfamiliar shape of the world around him.

His tiny fangs showed when his mouth opened.

Victor waited for a cry.

None came.

Instead, Zee settled against his chest.

Crimson Mana touched Victor’s hands.

It did not burn.

The king looked down at the heir he had waited years to meet.

“My son,” he said quietly.

Aurelia watched him.

For that moment, the blood outside did not matter.

The foreign riders did not matter.

The maddened wolves, waking vampires, and frightened witches seemed far beyond the chamber.

Victor held his child.

Then the first war horn sounded.

It came from the eastern tower.

A second answered from the southern wall.

The hatchling sprang upright, releasing a sharp cry as teal-and-gold mana flickered across its scales. Zee startled in Victor’s arms. A pulse of Crimson Mana struck the room, rattling every window.

Victor handed the child back to Aurelia and reached for his sword.

The chamber doors opened.

Captain Edric Vale, commander of the Darkheart Guard, entered with blood still staining the lower edge of his cloak.

“My King.”

“What has reached us?”

“Messengers. Not soldiers.”

“Then why sound the war horns?”

“Because there are twelve of them.”

Victor’s expression hardened.

“From whom?”

“Twelve crowns.”

Silence followed.

Aurelia held Zee closer.

Captain Vale continued. “They arrived separately, but each carries the same demand.”

Victor stepped toward him.

“Speak it.”

The captain hesitated.

“They want the child surrendered before nightfall.”

The crimson hatchling growled.

It was a weak, newborn sound, but the intention behind it was unmistakable.

Victor’s face darkened.

“On whose authority?”

“Theirs.”

“They believe twelve seals make them rulers of my house?”

“No, Your Majesty.”

“What do they threaten?”

“War.”

Aurelia looked toward Victor. “They would invade over a child born less than a night ago?”

“They are not invading over a child,” Victor said.

The words left him more harshly than he intended.

“They are invading because they believe fear gives them the right.”

He turned to Captain Vale.

“Send the messengers to the great hall. Feed them. Do not harm them.”

“And our answer?”

Victor looked back at Zee.

His child rested beneath Aurelia’s hand while the crimson hatchling remained beside him, watching the armed men.

“No foreign king decides what happens to the heir of House Darkheart.”

Captain Vale struck his fist to his chest.

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

When he departed, Aurelia’s shoulders eased.

“You will defend him.”

Victor looked toward the windows.

“I will defend this kingdom.”

The difference escaped her.

It did not escape him.

By midday, the twelve messengers stood beneath the vaulted ceiling of the great hall.

Each wore the colors of a different kingdom.

Some came from nations that had fought House Darkheart for generations. Others represented crowns bound to Victor through trade, marriage, or uneasy peace. Two served kings who had been at war with one another less than a week earlier.

Fear had made allies where diplomacy failed.

Victor sat upon the black throne.

The iron crown of House Darkheart rested upon his head. Generals and court witches stood along the walls. Captain Vale remained at the foot of the dais.

The first messenger stepped forward.

“My sovereign offers peace if the unnatural child is surrendered.”

Victor’s hand tightened around the throne.

The second spoke. “Our northern villages were destroyed by wolves at the exact hour of his birth.”

A third added, “Every witch in our capital became helpless.”

“Temporarily,” one of the Darkheart witches said. “Their magic was never gone.”

The messenger ignored her.

“A heartbeat entered the blood of every vampire beneath our mountains.”

Another voice rose from the line. “A child with witch mana, vampire fangs, and the scent of a wolf cannot be permitted to live.”

Victor stood.

The hall fell silent.

“You entered my kingdom beneath banners of peace,” he said. “You were fed at my table and permitted to stand within my throne room. Do not mistake restraint for weakness.”

The first messenger held his ground. “Then you refuse?”

“I refuse to surrender my son to frightened rulers who have not seen him.”

“They have seen the sky.”

“So have I.”

“They have buried those killed by the wolves.”

“So will I.”

“The child caused it.”

“You do not know that.”

“Neither do you.”

The words struck harder than Victor expected.

He descended one step from the throne.

The messenger continued. “Kill him yourself, and the armies will turn back.”

Aurelia’s voice came from the entrance.

“No.”

Everyone turned.

The queen entered carrying Zee. She should not have left the royal chamber, but there was no weakness in her posture. The crimson hatchling moved beside her in the arms of a nervous servant, struggling until it was placed near Zee again.

Aurelia stopped before the gathered envoys.

“You crossed our borders and entered my home to demand the murder of my newborn son.”

The first messenger lowered his head, though not in apology.

“My queen, we demand survival.”

“You demand obedience.”

The hatchling pressed against Zee, its teal-and-gold mana barely visible beneath its crimson scales.

One messenger stared at them.

“There are reports from the Dragon Kingdom.”

Victor looked toward him.

“What reports?”

“A royal egg hatched during the Blood Rain. The hatchling vanished from a sealed mountain chamber.”

Every eye shifted toward the small dragon beside Zee.

The creature buried its head against the infant.

The messenger whispered, “That may be what was taken.”

“No one here took him,” Aurelia said.

“Yet he came to the child.”

Another murmur moved through the hall.

Fear gained shape with every unanswered question.

Victor raised one hand.

“You have my answer. Leave Castle Darkheart before sunset.”

The first messenger did not move.

“Our armies are already gathering.”

“Then gather them farther from my walls.”

“If we return without the child, they will march.”

Victor’s voice lowered.

“Then they will die.”

The messengers were escorted from the hall.

Aurelia remained beside the throne after the doors closed.

“You meant it.”

Victor looked at the empty space where the envoys had stood.

“They threatened House Darkheart.”

“They threatened Zee.”

“They threatened both.”

She studied him.

“Does that distinction matter?”

Victor turned toward his son.

Zee’s crimson eyes were open again. The child reached toward him.

Victor did not take his hand this time.

Before Aurelia could question him, an old servant entered through a side door carrying a narrow wooden box.

He was blind in one eye and walked with the aid of an iron staff. Victor recognized him as the keeper of the royal burial vaults, a man who had served Victor’s father before Victor wore the crown.

The servant knelt.

“Forgive me, Your Majesty.”

“What is it?”

“Something opened beneath the castle.”

Victor’s attention sharpened.

“What opened?”

“A sealed chamber beneath the oldest crypt.”

“By whose command?”

“No living command.”

The servant raised the wooden box.

“This was inside.”

The object was carved from black wood and bound with rusted iron. No royal crest marked it. No witch sigil protected it. A strip of human skin had been wrapped around its center and tied with a knot darkened by age.

Aurelia recoiled.

“Remove that from my son.”

The servant remained kneeling.

“My Queen, the chamber opened when the prince was born.”

Victor looked toward Captain Vale. “Take Aurelia back to the nursery.”

“I am not leaving.”

“Aurelia.”

She heard the command in his voice.

For the first time since Zee’s birth, she saw something colder beneath Victor’s expression.

Not hatred.

Not yet.

Fear searching for an answer.

Aurelia held Zee more tightly.

“You will tell me what is inside that box.”

“When I know.”

She wanted to argue.

Then Zee stirred, and the crimson hatchling released a restless sound.

Aurelia finally turned away.

Victor watched her leave with their son.

The great hall doors closed.

“Open it,” he ordered.

The burial keeper placed the box upon the stone floor.

Captain Vale stood near Victor with one hand on his sword.

The old man cut through the strip of skin.

The knot loosened by itself.

A smell of earth, ash, and old blood entered the hall.

Inside the box rested no ancient book.

No record of a previous Tribrid.

No explanation of the child.

There was only a narrow piece of bone etched with words that looked freshly carved.

Victor crouched beside it.

“Who wrote this?”

“No one living,” the keeper whispered.

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I possess.”

Victor lifted the bone.

The writing darkened beneath his touch.

A voice entered the silent hall.

Not spoken by the burial keeper.

Not spoken by Captain Vale.

It came from the bone itself, thin and broken, as though carried through the throat of something long dead.

“When blood falls upward and beasts forget their names, a child of crown and hunger shall open his eyes beneath the blackened throne.”

Victor’s grip tightened.

The voice continued.

“Three lives shall breathe within one flesh, and every blade raised against him shall sharpen what it seeks to destroy.”

Captain Vale stepped backward.

The burial keeper began praying.

Victor could not move.

“He shall not inherit the world as it was given.”

The final words emerged slowly.

“The world shall break before it teaches him how to die.”

The bone split in Victor’s hand.

Silence returned.

No name had been spoken.

No future victory revealed.

No explanation given for Zee’s Crimson Mana, the Blood Rain, or the child’s impossible nature.

Yet Victor saw his son in every word.

Blood falling from the heavens.

Wolves losing themselves.

Three lives inside one body.

Every blade making him harder to destroy.

Victor stared at the fragments in his palm.

“Who knew this prophecy?”

The burial keeper shook his head.

“No one, Your Majesty. The chamber had no door yesterday.”

“Who placed the box there?”

“I do not know.”

“Was it my father?”

“No.”

“My grandfather?”

“No record names it.”

Victor rose.

The king who had threatened twelve kingdoms only moments earlier was gone.

In his place stood a father who had been handed a reason to fear his own child.

“Leave us,” he said.

The burial keeper bowed and retreated.

Captain Vale remained.

“My King—”

“Leave.”

The captain obeyed.

Victor stood alone in the great hall.

Outside, ravens carried reports across the ancient world.

Twelve kingdoms called their banners.

Wolves still wandered without reason through blood-soaked forests.

Vampires hunted for the source of the heartbeat that had entered their blood.

Witches whispered about the newborn whose presence had buried their magic beneath impossible pressure.

Inside the royal nursery, Aurelia laid Zee in his cradle.

The crimson hatchling curled beside him.

Zee reached through the simple bond between them.

Unease.

The hatchling lifted his head and stared toward the distant throne room.

Victor remained there until the final light disappeared from the windows.

When he eventually returned to the nursery, Aurelia looked up with relief.

“You found your answer?”

Victor stood in the doorway.

His eyes settled upon Zee.

The child smiled at the sight of him.

Victor remembered holding him.

He remembered the tiny hand around his finger.

Then the prophecy whispered again inside his mind.

Every blade raised against him shall sharpen what it seeks to destroy.

Victor did not approach the cradle.

“No,” he said.

Aurelia frowned. “No?”

“I found something worse.”

That night, the armies began marching toward House Darkheart.

And for the first time since Zee’s birth, his father looked at him and felt afraid to love him.
