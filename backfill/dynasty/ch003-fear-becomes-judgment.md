Chapter Three

Fear Becomes Judgment

By sunrise, the roads surrounding House Darkheart were no longer empty.

Scouts returned mud-soaked and sleepless, carrying reports from every direction. Columns of soldiers had been sighted beyond the eastern ridge. Siege wagons moved through the southern valleys. Northern banners had crossed the river before dawn, and riders from the west had begun seizing villages to establish supply lines.

Twelve kingdoms had not yet formed a true alliance.

They did not trust one another enough for that.

But fear had given them something almost as dangerous.

A shared destination.

Castle Darkheart.

The fortress woke beneath the sound of preparation.

Blacksmiths worked without rest. Arrowheads piled beside the walls. Witches renewed ancient defenses carved into stone generations earlier. Soldiers dragged barrels of oil toward murder holes and reinforced gates already thick enough to survive rams. Messengers rode through the inner city calling every reserve banner back to the capital.

House Darkheart had survived wars before.

This felt different.

No one could say whether the armies came for conquest, survival, religious terror, or simple panic.

Most did not care.

They were coming.

That was enough.

King Victor Darkheart stood over the war table in silence.

Wooden markers surrounded the carved representation of his kingdom.

Too many.

Captain Vale remained across from him while generals argued over roads, troop strength, and which kingdom would likely strike first.

Victor barely heard them.

The prophecy remained inside his head.

Not all of it.

One line.

Every blade raised against him shall sharpen what it seeks to destroy.

He had repeated it so many times through the night that the words no longer sounded like language.

They had become fear.

“Your Majesty?”

Victor looked up.

The High Marshal was waiting.

“Say it again.”

“The southern coalition is moving fastest. If they continue at their current pace, their advance force reaches our border settlements by tomorrow night.”

“And the east?”

“Two days.”

“The north?”

“Difficult terrain. Perhaps three.”

Victor nodded.

“Evacuate the villages closest to the border.”

The marshal hesitated. “Into the capital?”

“Into the fortified interior. Leave nothing useful behind.”

“Food stores?”

“Take what we can. Burn what we cannot.”

Several generals exchanged looks.

Victor saw them.

“Do you have a better way to feed an invading army?”

“No, Your Majesty.”

“Then carry out the order.”

The marshal bowed.

Victor placed both hands upon the table.

“Every gate closes by sundown. No foreign traveler enters after that. No merchant leaves with military information. Double the watch around the western tunnels and place witches at every old passage beneath the castle.”

Captain Vale glanced toward him.

“The nursery as well?”

The room went quiet.

Victor’s eyes moved to him.

“Especially the nursery.”

One of the generals shifted uneasily.

“Protection for the prince?”

Victor answered too slowly.

“Yes.”

The word came without warmth.

Captain Vale heard it.

No one else challenged the king.

The orders continued.

But the nursery remained in Victor’s thoughts.

Not because he feared someone would reach Zee before House Darkheart could protect him.

Because he had begun to fear what would happen if no one ever could.

—

Queen Aurelia refused to remain in bed.

The physicians protested.

She ignored them.

By midday, she sat in the royal nursery wrapped in a heavy black robe, Zee cradled against her chest while the crimson hatchling slept near her feet.

The newborn dragon had eaten poorly.

No one knew what to feed him.

The kitchens tried raw meat, cooked meat, milk, blood, fish, and even crushed gemstone offered by a terrified servant who claimed dragons ate treasure.

The hatchling rejected nearly everything until Zee began crying from hunger.

The sound changed him.

His little head lifted.

A simple feeling moved across the strange connection between them.

Hunger.

Not his.

Zee’s.

The hatchling climbed clumsily toward the queen and nudged her arm.

Aurelia stared.

“You know.”

The hatchling chirped.

She did not understand the bond.

She only understood what she saw.

He knew Zee needed something.

Aurelia fed her son.

The hatchling settled again.

When Zee relaxed, so did he.

Aurelia smiled faintly.

“There is more sense between the two of you than in half the royal court.”

The door opened.

Victor entered.

He had not changed clothes since the previous night.

Aurelia’s smile faded.

“You look terrible.”

“I have twelve kingdoms marching toward my walls.”

“You had twelve kingdoms marching toward your walls yesterday.”

“Yesterday they were threatening.”

“And now?”

“They are moving siege equipment.”

Aurelia looked down at Zee.

The child slept peacefully.

“Because of him.”

Victor said nothing.

Aurelia studied his face.

Something had changed.

Not suddenly.

But undeniably.

“You have not touched him since last night.”

Victor looked toward the cradle.

The hatchling opened its eyes.

A low warning sound rolled from its tiny chest.

Victor’s jaw tightened.

“That creature does that every time I enter.”

“It does it when armed men enter.”

“I am always armed.”

“Perhaps that is the problem.”

Victor nearly smiled.

He did not.

Aurelia noticed.

“What did you find beneath the castle?”

He remained silent.

“Victor.”

“The burial keeper found something that should not have been there.”

“What?”

“A prophecy.”

Aurelia’s expression hardened.

“No.”

“You have not heard it.”

“I do not need to hear it.”

Victor stepped closer.

“It spoke of the Blood Rain.”

Aurelia’s fingers tightened around Zee.

“It spoke of wolves losing themselves.”

“Stop.”

“It spoke of three lives within one body.”

“Stop.”

Victor did.

The room fell silent.

Aurelia looked at him with anger now.

“Do not bring old bones into my child’s cradle and call them truth.”

“You think I want this?”

“I think you are frightened.”

“I am.”

“Then say that.”

Victor looked at Zee.

The child stirred.

“Every army outside these walls is frightened too.”

“They do not know him.”

“Neither do we.”

“He is our son.”

“That does not answer what he is.”

Aurelia stood too quickly and winced.

Victor instinctively reached toward her.

She stepped back.

“Do not.”

His hand lowered.

“You held him yesterday,” she said. “You looked at him and saw your child.”

“I still see my child.”

“No. You are trying not to.”

Victor’s expression hardened.

“The prophecy says—”

“The prophecy does not feed him.”

“Aurelia.”

“It does not know how he sleeps. It does not know the sound he makes when he is hungry. It does not know that he reaches for you.”

Victor looked away.

That hurt more than she intended.

She continued anyway.

“You want something ancient to tell you who your son is because you are afraid to learn for yourself.”

Victor’s voice lowered.

“You did not hear the final line.”

“I do not care.”

“I do.”

Aurelia’s eyes flashed.

“That is what frightens me.”

The hatchling rose between them.

Teal-and-gold mana shimmered along his crimson scales.

He did not understand the argument.

He understood fear.

Zee felt it through him.

The newborn’s Crimson Mana stirred.

The lamps flickered.

Victor stepped back.

Not because the mana threatened him.

Because the movement confirmed something inside him.

Even as an infant, Zee responded.

Adapted.

Reacted.

The prophecy whispered again.

Every blade raised against him shall sharpen what it seeks to destroy.

Victor looked at the crimson glow around his son.

For the first time, he wondered whether love itself might be another blade.

And whether mercy could become the cruelty that doomed them all.

—

That evening, the first foreign campfires became visible from Castle Darkheart.

Hundreds at first.

Then thousands.

They burned along the distant plains like a second constellation beneath the horizon.

Citizens gathered along the inner walls to watch.

No one celebrated.

No one panicked openly either.

House Darkheart had survived too much for that.

But fear moved through the capital in quiet ways.

Shops closed early.

Parents kept children indoors.

Shrines filled with offerings.

Old swords were pulled from family chests.

Witches prepared healing rooms in temples and military halls.

Rumors multiplied faster than soldiers.

Some said the prince had been born drinking blood.

Others claimed he had transformed into a wolf before his first breath.

One story insisted the dragon hatchling had bowed to him.

Another said every vampire in the east now carried Zee’s heartbeat.

None were true.

That did not matter.

Fear rarely waited for truth.

Within the war chamber, Victor listened as Captain Vale read the newest demands.

“Three kingdoms still request surrender.”

“Request?”

“Demand.”

“Then call it what it is.”

Captain Vale continued.

“Four demand execution witnessed by neutral envoys.”

Victor’s face did not change.

“Neutral.”

“Yes.”

“There are no neutral crowns left.”

“No, Your Majesty.”

“And the others?”

“Two demand the queen and child be removed from the royal line.”

Victor’s hand tightened.

“On what authority?”

“None that House Darkheart recognizes.”

“The remaining three?”

Captain Vale paused.

“They have stopped sending demands.”

Victor looked up.

“Why?”

“They are preparing to attack.”

The council went silent.

Victor walked toward the window.

From there he could see faint fires beyond the hills.

“Twelve kingdoms,” he said.

Captain Vale nodded.

“Against one newborn.”

“No.”

Victor turned.

“Against House Darkheart.”

Several nobles exchanged relieved looks.

It sounded like pride.

Sovereignty.

Defiance.

The king they understood.

Then Victor added, “And they will not decide his fate.”

The room changed.

Captain Vale heard the difference immediately.

Victor no longer said they would not kill Zee.

He said they would not decide.

That distinction was becoming larger every hour.

—

Later that night, Victor returned alone to the burial chamber.

The broken bone remained where it had split.

He had ordered no one to touch it.

The chamber smelled colder now.

He knelt and gathered the pieces.

No voice spoke.

No new words appeared.

The prophecy had given what it intended to give.

Nothing more.

Victor stared at the fragments.

“What are you?”

Silence.

“What does he become?”

Nothing answered.

Victor laughed once.

There was no humor in it.

“You tell me enough to fear him and not enough to know why.”

He looked toward the ceiling as though he could see through stone into the nursery above.

“My son.”

The words nearly broke him.

He closed his eyes.

He remembered Zee’s hand around his finger.

The small weight against his chest.

Aurelia’s smile.

Then he imagined the armies outside the walls multiplied a hundred times.

A thousand.

He imagined every kingdom someday falling.

Not because the prophecy promised it.

Because Victor’s fear supplied the rest.

That was how the prophecy began winning.

Not by revealing the future.

By forcing a frightened man to imagine one.

Victor opened his eyes.

“If they attack him…”

The line returned.

Every blade raised against him shall sharpen what it seeks to destroy.

His breathing slowed.

The problem was no longer only that Zee might survive an attempt on his life.

The problem was what survival might do to him.

Victor began to think like a king confronting an enemy.

Quietly.

Methodically.

Terribly.

He could not allow foreign rulers to test the prophecy.

He could not allow witches, vampires, wolves, or armies to discover whether hurting Zee truly made him harder to destroy.

He could not allow anyone else to control the experiment.

The thought arrived so naturally that Victor did not recognize how far he had fallen until it was already complete.

If the prophecy must be tested…

His stomach turned.

He stood abruptly.

“No.”

The word echoed through the chamber.

For several breaths, he fought himself.

Then he whispered,

“Not yet.”

That was worse.

—

In the nursery above, Zee woke.

No sound had reached him.

No spell touched the room.

Yet discomfort spread through his small body.

The crimson hatchling opened his eyes at the same moment.

Danger.

Not a place.

Not a face.

Only the instinctive feeling of something wrong.

The hatchling crawled closer.

Zee’s tiny hand found his scales.

Safe.

The feeling passed between them.

Small.

Simple.

Desperate.

Outside the nursery, two additional guards arrived under direct royal order.

Then two more.

By midnight, no one could enter the corridor without Victor’s permission.

Aurelia believed the king had increased protection because foreign armies were approaching.

She slept beside her son without knowing the truth.

Victor was not only keeping the world away from Zee.

He was beginning to keep Zee where he could control him.

By the end of the night, the child in Victor’s mind had become two things at once.

His son.

And the prophecy wearing his son’s face.
