Aidan woke to darkness.

Not underground.

The infirmary.

Rain tapped softly against the windows.

The familiar scent of herbs lingered in the air.

Every part of his body hurt.

For one brief moment…

He almost welcomed it.

Pain meant he was still alive.

The thought no longer comforted him.

…

“You should still be unconscious.”

Helena’s voice broke the silence.

She stood beside his bed, exhaustion etched into every line of her face.

“You’ve been asleep for almost two days.”

Aidan tried to sit.

The room spun violently.

Pain tore through his ribs.

His side burned beneath layers of fresh bandages.

His shoulder refused to lift higher than his chest.

Helena gently pushed him back.

“No.”

“I have patrol.”

“No.”

“The rescue reports.”

“No.”

“The northern villages—”

“No.”

Every answer came firmer than the last.

“You almost died.”

Silence.

Finally Aidan asked the question that truly mattered.

“The apprentices?”

“They’re alive.”

His shoulders relaxed.

Only then.

Helena noticed.

He had asked about everyone else before himself.

Again.

…

Later that afternoon…

Rowan entered alone.

Neither spoke immediately.

The old master quietly placed another weathered map beside the bed.

Three fresh marks.

Three separate villages.

“These came while you were unconscious.”

Aidan closed his eyes.

“How bad?”

“One caravan disappeared.”

He moved one marker.

“A bridge collapsed.”

Another.

“And an entire watch patrol never returned.”

The last marker remained untouched.

“We haven’t found them.”

Aidan slowly sat upright despite Helena’s orders.

Every movement hurt.

He ignored it.

“I’ll go.”

“No.”

Rowan’s answer came without hesitation.

“You can barely breathe.”

“They still need someone.”

“They need you alive.”

Aidan looked toward the rain outside.

“If I stay here…”

He swallowed.

“…someone else won’t.”

The room fell silent.

Neither man won.

Neither man was wrong.

…

By evening…

Aidan had left the infirmary anyway.

His pace was slower.

His breathing shallower.

The academy noticed immediately.

Students stopped pretending not to stare.

Some smiled with relief.

Others looked frightened.

Not because he was injured.

Because they had begun believing he couldn’t be.

He walked past them quietly.

One first-year rushed forward.

“Master Aidan…”

He still wasn’t used to hearing that title.

“We repaired the eastern barrier.”

The young witch smiled proudly.

“We did it the way you taught us.”

For the first time in days…

Aidan almost smiled.

“Good.”

Only one word.

Yet the student beamed as though he’d received the greatest praise imaginable.

Aidan continued walking.

The moment passed.

His smile disappeared before anyone else noticed it.
